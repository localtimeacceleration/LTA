\begin{abstract}
The tension between local ($H_0 \approx 73$ km/s/Mpc) and early-universe ($H_0 \approx 67$ km/s/Mpc) 
measurements of the Hubble constant remains the defining crisis of modern cosmology. In this work, we 
investigate whether this tension can be ameliorated by a phenomenological ``Local Time Acceleration'' 
(LTA) parametrization, which modifies the observed redshift via a bounded, observer-anchored decay function. 
We fit this model to the Pantheon+SH0ES dataset using the full released covariance matrix and a 
ladder-anchored likelihood. We report a statistically significant preference for the LTA model over 
standard $\Lambda$CDM ($\Delta \chi^2 \approx 13.65$, corresponding to $\sim 3.7\sigma$), with a Monte 
Carlo calibrated tail probability $p_{\rm MC}\approx 8\times 10^{-4}$ under $N=10000$ baseline null 
injections. Tomographic analysis reveals that this signal is driven entirely by supernovae in the local 
volume ($z < 0.03$), effectively correcting a known ``dip'' in the Hubble residuals often attributed to 
peculiar velocities in the Local Void. Crucially, we perform a rigorous Jackknife resampling of all major 
constituent surveys (CfA, CSP, PS1, etc.) and find the signal is robust to the exclusion of any single 
telescope group, ruling out instrument-specific calibration systematics. While the effect vanishes at 
higher redshifts, its persistence and coherence in the local volume suggest that the local Hubble flow 
exhibits structured kinematic deviations that are better described by a smooth temporal decay than by 
standard peculiar velocity corrections alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The persistent mismatch between late-time (local) determinations of the Hubble constant
($H_0 \simeq 73\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$) and early-universe inferences under minimal flat
$\Lambda$CDM ($H_0 \simeq 67\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$) has become the central anomaly in
precision cosmology \cite{Brout2022PantheonPlus,Riess2022SH0ES}.
What makes the ``Hubble tension'' unusually stubborn is that it lives at the intersection of
multiple tightly constrained datasets: attempts to modify early-time physics must preserve the
CMB acoustic peak structure, while late-time modifications to the expansion history are strongly
constrained by the inverse distance ladder (BAO + SNe~Ia) once the sound horizon scale is fixed.

A less explored possibility is that part of the discrepancy is not best described as a global
change in the background expansion $H(z)$, but rather as a small, observer-linked distortion in the
mapping between observed redshift and the cosmological redshift that enters background distances.
This is not a semantic distinction: the SH0ES-style distance-ladder inference is dominated by
nearby Type~Ia supernovae and is therefore maximally sensitive to the detailed kinematics of the
local Hubble flow.
The observer resides in a structured environment (including a local underdensity often discussed
under the umbrella of ``Hubble bubble'' / ``Local Void'' phenomenology), and the lowest-redshift
portion of the Hubble diagram exhibits coherent residual structure that is not naturally captured
by purely stochastic peculiar-velocity scatter alone.
If the local Hubble flow contains an additional smooth, causal, line-of-sight effect that is not
modeled in the standard pipeline, it should manifest precisely in the redshift range where the
distance ladder is anchored and where local-flow systematics are most acute.

In this paper we introduce and test a phenomenological extension of $\Lambda$CDM which we call
\emph{Local Time Acceleration} (LTA).
Operationally, LTA is implemented as an accumulated fractional frequency drift along the photon
trajectory, producing an additive contribution to $\ln(1+z)$ and therefore a multiplicative
correction to $(1+z)$:
\begin{equation}
1+\zobs = (1+\zcos)\exp[I(\chi)].
\end{equation}
The defining modeling choice is that the drift strength is evaluated using an \emph{Earth-retarded
lookback time} argument, so that the effect is explicitly causal and tied to the observer's
lightcone rather than to a global function of the scale factor.
We further impose boundedness (the drift saturates and becomes negligible beyond a finite
activation epoch) and monotonicity of the mapping (so that $\zcos(\zobs)$ remains single-valued),
making the model both numerically well-posed and falsifiable.

Our goal in this work is deliberately specific: \emph{does a causal, bounded, observer-anchored
redshift mapping of the LTA type improve the joint fit to late-time distance--redshift data when
those data are analyzed using their released full covariances?}
We emphasize what this statement does and does not entail.
A detection of a preference for LTA would be an empirical statement about the adequacy of a
particular mapping for describing the Hubble diagram and BAO observables; it would not, by itself,
uniquely identify a microphysical origin.
At the same time, this is not an unconstrained curve-fitting exercise: the mapping is nested
($s_{\rm anchor}\to 0$ recovers $\Lambda$CDM exactly), it is restricted by causality and
boundedness, and it is tested jointly against BAO, whose radial observable depends directly on the
redshift Jacobian implied by the mapping.

Our analysis is intentionally conservative in data handling.
For SNe~Ia we use the Pantheon+SH0ES public release \cite{Brout2022PantheonPlus,Riess2022SH0ES}, restricting to the
\texttt{shoes\_global} selection and adopting the released \emph{full} covariance matrix, including
the nontrivial cross-correlations between calibrators and Hubble-flow supernovae.
Because calibrator/Hubble-flow correlations are substantial, we construct a ladder-anchored SN
likelihood in which calibrators provide an absolute-distance anchor and the cosmology-sensitive
Hubble-flow likelihood is obtained by conditioning on calibrators via a Schur complement
(Sec.~\ref{sec:sn_anchor_like}).
We combine this with a BAO consensus likelihood (Sec.~\ref{sec:bao_like})\cite{DESI2024BAO}, treating the sound
horizon through a single scaling parameter $\alpha_{\rm rd}$.
Crucially, BAO provides an independent lever arm on the mapping: the LTA redshift Jacobian modifies
the radial BAO prediction in a way that a purely SN-driven distortion generally cannot evade.

For inference we compare baseline flat $\Lambda$CDM against $\Lambda$CDM+LTA under the same data
treatment and numerical safeguards.
Because the LTA extension is nested and we impose physical bounds on the LTA amplitude, the null is
a boundary point and standard asymptotic $\chi^2$-difference heuristics are not guaranteed to be
reliable.

Because the LTA amplitude is constrained (e.g.\ \(s_{\rm anchor}\ge 0\)), the null is a boundary
point and standard Wilks-theorem \(\chi^2\) asymptotics need not hold; in simple one-sided cases the
likelihood-ratio statistic approaches a nonstandard mixture distribution rather than \(\chi^2_1\)
\cite{Chernoff1954,SelfLiang1987}.
In astronomy/cosmology contexts this issue is well known in the detection of additional model
components and motivates Monte Carlo calibration when regularity conditions fail \cite{Protassov2002}.
We therefore treat the parametric bootstrap as the primary calibration for \(\Delta\chi^2\) in this
paper (Sec.~\ref{sec:null_bootstrap}).

We therefore calibrate the detection significance using a Monte Carlo parametric bootstrap (null
injections) under the baseline model, generating mock realizations with the \emph{same} released
covariances and refitting both models end-to-end (Sec.~\ref{sec:opt_stat} and
Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}).
In addition, we perform falsification-oriented robustness tests, including leave-one-group-out
(jackknife) refits by telescope/survey grouping to check whether the preference is driven by any
single subset, and supplementary cross-validation diagnostics designed for strongly correlated data
(App.~\ref{app:cv}).

\section{Context: Relation to existing approaches to the Hubble tension}
\label{sec:related_work}

The discrepancy between late-time determinations of the Hubble constant and early-universe inferences
under minimal flat $\Lambda$CDM has generated an exceptionally broad literature.
Comprehensive reviews emphasize that proposed resolutions fall into three overlapping categories:
(i) unrecognized systematics in one or more datasets and inference pipelines;
(ii) new physics that modifies early-universe calibration, typically by reducing the sound horizon
$r_d$ and thereby raising the CMB-inferred $H_0$; and (iii) new physics (or unmodeled astrophysics)
that modifies the late-time distance--redshift relation or the interpretation of low-$z$ anchors
(e.g.\ dynamical dark energy, modified gravity, or local-structure effects)
\cite{DiValentino2021Review,Verde2019Tensions}.
Because the observational inputs are heterogeneous (SNe~Ia, Cepheids/TRGB, BAO, CMB anisotropies,
lensing, time delays, masers, and standard sirens), it is useful to state explicitly where the LTA
framework tested here sits within this landscape, and how it relates to the failure modes that have
been most discussed.

At a high level, the LTA hypothesis explored in this paper is \emph{not} an attempt to modify the
global expansion history $H(z)$ as a free function, nor is it designed to alter recombination-era
physics to shift $r_d$.
Instead, it is a phenomenological proposal that the mapping from observed redshift to the
background cosmological redshift entering homogeneous FLRW distances may acquire an additional
\emph{observer-anchored, causal, bounded} contribution in the local Universe.
This places LTA closest in spirit to the class of explanations in which part (or all) of the SH0ES--
Planck discrepancy is driven by effects that are specific to the \emph{local Hubble-flow anchor
regime}, including local kinematics, local structure, or other observer-linked biases that are not
captured by standard peculiar-velocity modeling.

\subsection{Early-universe resolutions: shifting the sound horizon}
A large fraction of proposed ``new physics'' solutions aim to reduce the sound horizon at the drag
epoch, $r_d$, while preserving the excellent fit of $\Lambda$CDM to the CMB angular power spectra
and other early-time observables.
If $r_d$ is smaller than in minimal $\Lambda$CDM, then the inverse distance ladder (BAO + SNe~Ia),
when anchored by CMB physics, can accommodate a larger inferred $H_0$.
Representative ideas include early dark energy (EDE) episodes that briefly contribute non-negligible
energy density near matter--radiation equality \cite{Poulin2019EDE}, additional relativistic degrees
of freedom (larger $N_{\rm eff}$), modifications to recombination, and scenarios in which neutrino
physics is altered (e.g.\ self-interactions) in ways that reshape the CMB damping tail and inferred
parameters \cite{Kreisch2019NeutrinoPuzzle}.
The review literature emphasizes that these models are tightly constrained: they must preserve the
CMB peak structure, lensing, and BBN consistency, and they are increasingly challenged by the
combined CMB+BAO+large-scale-structure dataset hierarchy \cite{DiValentino2021Review}.

The LTA analysis in this paper is largely orthogonal to this program.
Operationally, we introduce an explicit sound-horizon scaling parameter $\alpha_{\rm rd}$ in the BAO
likelihood, and we test whether an \emph{observer-anchored redshift mapping} can improve the fit in
the regime that dominates local distance-ladder anchoring, without requiring a shift of early-time
physics.
Consequently, if future work finds a residual tension after accounting for observer-local effects,
LTA-like mappings could in principle be combined with early-time modifications rather than being in
direct competition with them.
Conversely, if an early-time solution fully resolves the discrepancy across all late-time probes,
it would predict that no additional observer-local mapping distortion is required; this provides a
clear empirical discriminant.

\subsection{Late-universe modifications: dynamical dark energy and modified gravity}
A second broad class of proposals modifies late-time cosmology by changing the expansion history or
the growth of structure relative to minimal $\Lambda$CDM.
These include dynamical dark energy parameterizations ($w\neq -1$, evolving $w(z)$), interacting dark
sector models, and modified-gravity theories in which the relation between geometry and
stress--energy differs from GR on cosmological scales \cite{DiValentino2021Review,Verde2019Tensions}.
Such models are constrained not only by the SNe~Ia Hubble diagram but also by BAO (both transverse
and radial), weak lensing, redshift-space distortions, cluster abundances, and CMB lensing.

The empirical signature isolated in this paper differs from what these models typically target.
Our best-fit LTA profiles are deliberately \emph{bounded and saturating}, and the improvement is
tomographically localized to $z\lesssim 0.03$, leaving the best-fit background $(H_0,\Omega_m)$ and
the higher-$z$ Hubble diagram close to baseline.
This behavior is more naturally interpreted as a correction to the \emph{local} mapping between
$\zobs$ and the effective redshift argument used in distances, rather than as a genuine modification
of $H(z)$ over a broad redshift lever arm.
Accordingly, LTA does not aim to replace late-time dark energy phenomenology globally; rather, it
tests whether the low-$z$ ladder anchor regime exhibits a structured deviation that is better
captured by a smooth, causal, observer-local mapping than by standard local-flow scatter models.

\subsection{Systematics and independent late-time anchors}
A persistent theme in the literature is that the SH0ES--Planck discrepancy could reflect residual
systematics in one or more components of the distance ladder (Cepheid calibration, SN~Ia
standardization, selection effects, dust, host correlations) or in early-universe inference.
For this reason, independent determinations of $H_0$ have been heavily emphasized.
These include TRGB-calibrated ladders \cite{Freedman2019TRGB}, strong-lensing time-delay cosmography
\cite{Wong2019H0LiCOW,Birrer2020TDCOSMOIV}, geometric megamaser distances \cite{Pesce2020Megamaser},
and gravitational-wave standard sirens \cite{Abbott2017StandardSiren}.
A key point from review work is that the most informative cross-checks are those that change both
the astrophysical systematics and the inference architecture, rather than merely reprocessing the
same ladder ingredients \cite{DiValentino2021Review,Verde2019Tensions}.

The contribution of this paper is not to adjudicate between all of these external $H_0$ anchors.
Instead, we take the Pantheon+SH0ES public release at face value and analyze it using the published
\emph{full} covariance structure (including calibrator--Hubble-flow cross-correlations), and we ask
a narrower question: within that released statistical model, is there evidence for a local,
smoothly varying mapping distortion that improves the Hubble-flow residual structure, and does that
distortion survive falsification-oriented robustness tests (jackknife, null injections) and remain
consistent with BAO once the mapping Jacobian is propagated?
In that sense, LTA should be read as an \emph{internal-consistency hypothesis test} on a specific
late-time dataset combination, rather than as an attempt to supersede the full cross-probe
literature on $H_0$.

\subsection{Local structure, cosmic variance, and redshift interpretation}
A third major theme, directly relevant to the present work, is whether the local environment of the
observer can bias low-$z$ determinations of $H_0$.
This includes (i) the possibility that the observer resides in a local underdensity or otherwise
atypical region, affecting inferred distances and velocities (``Hubble bubble'' / Local Void
phenomenology), and (ii) irreducible cosmic variance in the locally measured Hubble flow even in a
perfect experiment \cite{WuHuterer2017SampleVariance}.
Detailed analyses in the SN context have generally concluded that a simple spherically symmetric
local void of realistic amplitude is insufficient, by itself, to reconcile SH0ES with Planck
\cite{Kenworthy2019LocalPerspective}, though local-flow modeling and sample selection remain active
topics.

LTA is deliberately aligned with the \emph{spirit} of this class of explanations—namely, that the
anchor regime of the distance ladder may encode structured local effects—but it differs in its
implementation.
Rather than modeling a particular density profile or velocity field, we introduce a nested,
bounded, monotonic mapping in which photons acquire an accumulated contribution to $\ln(1+z)$
parameterized as a line-of-sight integral and evaluated with an Earth-retarded time argument.
This choice yields a concrete, testable prediction beyond a generic ``local effect'' statement:
the mapping implies a redshift Jacobian $\dd\zobs/\dd\zcos$ that directly affects radial BAO.
In other words, LTA is not merely an empirical correction to the SN Hubble diagram; it is a mapping
whose consistency can be independently checked in observables that depend on redshift derivatives.

\subsection{What is novel in the present analysis}
Within the broader literature, the main methodological distinctions of this work are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{A specific observer-anchored redshift-mapping ansatz.}
We implement the hypothesis as a multiplicative correction to $(1+z)$ (additive in $\ln(1+z)$),
accumulated along the photon trajectory, with explicit bounded activation and an Earth-retarded
time construction to enforce causal bookkeeping.

\item \textbf{Full released-covariance treatment with calibrator cross-correlations respected.}
The Pantheon+SH0ES release contains nontrivial calibrator--Hubble-flow covariance coupling; we
construct a ladder-anchored likelihood via a Schur complement so that calibrators act as an
absolute-distance anchor without discarding the published correlation structure.

\item \textbf{Significance calibration under a boundary-nested null.}
Because the LTA amplitude is bounded ($s_{\rm anchor}\ge 0$), the null is a boundary point; we
therefore calibrate $\Delta\chi^2$ using parametric bootstrap null injections that preserve the
released covariance and rerun the full refitting pipeline.

\item \textbf{A joint SN+BAO test that propagates the mapping Jacobian.}
BAO is not used only as a late-time distance lever arm; it provides a falsification channel because
radial BAO depends explicitly on the redshift Jacobian implied by the mapping.

\item \textbf{Tomographic localization of the effect.}
The preference is driven by a narrow low-$z$ shell ($z\lesssim 0.03$), consistent with a local
kinematic/mapping effect and inconsistent with a broad modification of the late-time expansion law.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, these points place LTA in a well-defined position relative to the existing solution
space reviewed in \cite{DiValentino2021Review}: it is a \emph{phenomenological, observer-local mapping
extension} designed to address structured low-$z$ Hubble-flow residuals while remaining explicitly
testable against BAO through the Jacobian constraint.
If the preference survives further external scrutiny (alternative SN compilations, alternative flow
models, sky-split tests, independent anchors), then it motivates a broader program of checking
whether part of the Hubble tension is best understood as a local redshift-interpretation bias rather
than as a single global modification of early- or late-time cosmology.

\section{Conceptual Framework: Local Time Acceleration}
\label{sec:concept}

The motivating hypothesis behind this work is unconventional, so it behoves us to be explicit about
what is being proposed at the observational level, what requirements we impose to keep it
well-posed, and what claims the present analysis does and does not make.
Our aim here is not to argue for a specific microphysical origin, but to define a concrete and
falsifiable phenomenological mapping between cosmological redshift and observed redshift, and then
to test whether that mapping is preferred by late-time distance--redshift data analyzed using their
released covariances.

\subsection{LTA as an accumulated, observer-local contribution to $\ln(1+z)$}

In standard FLRW cosmology, the observed redshift is determined by the ratio of scale factors,
$1+z = a(t_{\rm obs})/a(t_{\rm emit})$, up to conventional local corrections (peculiar velocities,
gravitational potentials, etc.) that are treated as separate effects.
LTA modifies this assumption by introducing an additional \emph{line-of-sight accumulated} fractional
frequency drift. The observational statement is that photons acquire a small multiplicative factor
in $(1+z)$ beyond the cosmological contribution:
\begin{equation}
1+\zobs = (1+\zcos)\exp[I(\chi)].
\end{equation}
Writing the effect in $\ln(1+z)$ is not a stylistic choice: it is the natural representation for a
process that composes multiplicatively and accumulates additively along a trajectory.
The quantity $I(\chi)$ is constructed as the line-of-sight integral of an effective drift-rate
field $s(\chi)$ (with units of a Hubble rate), so that $s/c$ acts as a fractional frequency-drift
per unit comoving distance (Sec.~\ref{sec:lta_mapping}).

This is not a coordinate relabeling.
A pure coordinate transformation cannot change dimensionless local observables without additional
physical content.
In contrast, LTA is defined precisely as an additional physical contribution to the observed
frequency ratio relative to the baseline $\Lambda$CDM prediction, parameterized in a way that is
nested, bounded, and testable.

\subsection{Why an Earth-retarded time argument appears}

The distinctive structural element of the LTA ansatz is that the drift-rate profile is evaluated as
a function of an \emph{observer-retarded} lookback time \(\tret\), rather than as a generic function
of cosmic time or scale factor.
The purpose of this construction is causal bookkeeping: if the underlying chronometric coupling is
a \emph{universal} law but the dominant contribution in the present epoch is sourced by the
observer’s nearby environment, then any influence on the observed past light cone must be
consistent with propagation at or below the speed of light.
We therefore tie the effective drift strength at a point along the photon path to a retarded-time
assignment based on the observer worldline and the background geometry (Sec.~\ref{sec:tret2}).

Importantly, ``Earth-sourced'' here should be read as \emph{observer-anchored} rather than as a
commitment to a particular terrestrial mechanism.
The present analysis does not require (and does not assume) that the relevant state variable is
literally biological.
Instead, the Earth-retarded construction is a compact proxy for a wide class of observer-local
causal influences whose effective strength depends on the observer’s local history or environment.
The empirical question tested below is therefore: does an \emph{observer-anchored, causal, bounded}
mapping of this type improve the description of the data?

\subsection{Bounded activation and controlled degrees of freedom}

To remain physically viable and empirically falsifiable, the LTA contribution must not grow without
limit.
We therefore impose a bounded activation through a function $g(t)\in[0,1]$ such that the effect
decays to zero at a finite cutoff epoch $\tL$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:earth_history2}).
This ensures that the additional drift saturates and becomes negligible for sufficiently large
lookback times, preventing the model from contaminating high-redshift cosmology where early-universe
constraints are exceptionally tight.

A second requirement is that the redshift mapping remain monotonic:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \zobs}{\dd \zcos} > 0,
\end{equation}
so that the inverse relation $\zcos(\zobs)$ is single-valued and the Hubble diagram remains
well-defined (Sec.~\ref{sec:inverse2}).
This is not merely a numerical convenience: non-monotonic mappings would imply multi-valued distance
predictions at fixed observed redshift and would be directly incompatible with the interpretation of
both SNe~Ia and BAO as functions of redshift.

Finally, the model is explicitly \emph{nested}.
In the zero-amplitude limit ($s_{\rm anchor}\to 0$), the integral $I(\chi)\to 0$ and the mapping
reduces exactly to baseline $\Lambda$CDM.
This nesting allows us to compare $\Lambda$CDM and $\Lambda$CDM+LTA at fixed data treatment using
standard likelihood-ratio-style test statistics, while still calibrating their null distribution
via Monte Carlo because the null lies on a boundary (Sec.~\ref{sec:opt_stat}).

\subsection{Why BAO matters for falsification}

Although the primary low-redshift leverage comes from SNe~Ia, BAO plays a crucial conceptual role in
this test.
The LTA mapping implies not only a shift in the distance--redshift relation but also a redshift
Jacobian $\dd \zobs/\dd \zcos$.
Radial BAO observables depend explicitly on $\dd \chi/\dd z$ and therefore directly probe the
Jacobian induced by the mapping (Sec.~\ref{sec:bao_like}).
This provides an independent check against mappings that might otherwise fit SNe~Ia by distorting
redshift in a way that would be inconsistent with a standard ruler measured in the radial direction.
For this reason, joint SN+BAO fitting is an essential part of rendering the LTA hypothesis
falsifiable with late-time data.

\subsection{What this paper claims and what it does not}

The restricted empirical claim supported by this paper (if the preference persists under the full
set of robustness checks) is:
\emph{within the adopted data treatment and model family, an observer-anchored, causal, bounded LTA
mapping provides an improved fit to late-time distance--redshift data relative to baseline
$\Lambda$CDM.}

This is not a claim of unique microphysical identification.
The same phenomenology could in principle arise from unmodeled local kinematics, residual
calibration structure, or other observer-local effects not captured in the baseline pipeline.
Accordingly, our strategy is to (i) implement a precise mapping with clear causal and boundedness
requirements, (ii) fit it using the released full covariance structure (including calibrator/HF
cross-covariance), and (iii) report falsification-oriented diagnostics that test whether the
preference is distributed across the data, robust to leave-out refits, and unlikely under baseline
null injections.

In this sense, LTA is treated here as a \emph{testable phenomenological hypothesis}: the cosmology
data are used to evaluate whether the mapping is statistically preferred, while the interpretation
of its ultimate origin is left to independent constraints and future targeted tests
(Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Photon-frequency transport and the physical meaning of an integrated $\Delta\ln(1+z)$}
\label{sec:photon_transport}

This paper’s data analysis is built around an \emph{integrated} redshift modification,
\(\Delta\ln(1+z)\equiv I(\chi)\), and a corresponding local drift field \(s(\chi)\).
Because this structure is central (and is used directly in the BAO Jacobian), we make explicit here
how an integrated correction arises from standard photon-frequency transport once one posits a
universal chronometric coupling between \emph{organization} and \emph{realized clock time}.

\subsection{Redshift as a frequency ratio measured by matter clocks (Jordan frame)}
\label{sec:photon_transport_gr}

In geometric optics, a photon is described by a null wavevector \(k^\mu\) tangent to a null geodesic
of the \emph{physical} (matter) metric. In the Jordan-first completion adopted in this revision,
that physical metric is \(\gJ_{\mu\nu}\). Thus
\begin{equation}
k^\mu k_\mu = 0,
\qquad
k^\nu \nabla^{\mathrm{J}}_\nu k^\mu = 0,
\end{equation}
and the frequency measured by an observer with Jordan-frame 4-velocity \(u^\mu\) is
\begin{equation}
\nu_{\mathrm{J}} \propto -\,k_\mu u^\mu \quad (\text{contraction with } \gJ_{\mu\nu}).
\label{eq:nu_gr_def}
\end{equation}
Operationally, redshift is a \emph{ratio of measured frequencies}:
\begin{equation}
1+z_{\rm obs} \equiv \frac{\nu_{\rm emit,J}}{\nu_{\rm obs,J}}
= \frac{(-k_\mu u^\mu)_{\rm emit}}{(-k_\mu u^\mu)_{\rm obs}}.
\label{eq:z_gr_ratio}
\end{equation}

For comoving observers in an unperturbed Jordan-frame FLRW background,
\(\gJ_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu = -d\tJ^2 + \aJ^2(\tJ)d\bm{x}^2\),
Eq.~\eqref{eq:z_gr_ratio} yields the familiar scale-factor law
\begin{equation}
1+z_{\rm cos}=\frac{\aJ(\tJ_{\rm obs})}{\aJ(\tJ_{\rm emit})}.
\label{eq:z_cos_flrw}
\end{equation}
(See, e.g., standard GR/cosmology treatments \cite{Wald1984GR}.)

\subsection{Derived chronometric factor from Jordan-frame scalar--tensor structure}
\label{sec:photon_transport_chrono}

Earlier versions of this work introduced a universal chronometric factor \(A(\psi)\) as a direct
postulate relating ``realized clock time'' to a background proper time. In the Jordan-frame-first
scalar--tensor completion adopted here, the chronometric factor is \emph{derived} by the standard
conformal transformation between Jordan and Einstein frames.

\paragraph{Jordan vs.\ Einstein frames and a derived \(A\).}
Recall from Sec.~\ref{sec:conformal_completion} that the Einstein-frame metric is defined by
\(\gE_{\mu\nu}=\Fpsi\,\gJ_{\mu\nu}\), so that matter becomes conformally coupled in Einstein variables.
The conformal factor that appears in the matter coupling is
\begin{equation}
\Apsi \equiv \Fpsi^{-1/2},
\qquad
\Cpsi \equiv \ln \Apsi = -\frac{1}{2}\ln \Fpsi,
\label{eq:clk_factor_main}
\end{equation}
which is not an independent ingredient; it is fixed once \(F(\psi)\) is specified.

\paragraph{Clock-time relation (frame conversion).}
Jordan proper time is the physical clock time, while Einstein proper time is a computational
convenience. Along any worldline,
\begin{equation}
d\tau_{\mathrm{J}} = \Apsi\, d\tau_{\mathrm{E}}.
\label{eq:nu_meas_main}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Frequency conversion and redshift bookkeeping.}
A detector counts cycles \(N\) per unit proper time. Therefore the frequency measured by matter clocks
in the physical frame is
\begin{equation}
\nu_{\mathrm{J}} \equiv \frac{dN}{d\tau_{\mathrm{J}}}
= \frac{1}{\Apsi}\frac{dN}{d\tau_{\mathrm{E}}}
= \frac{1}{\Apsi}\,\nu_{\mathrm{E}}.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:z_gr_ratio}, the observed redshift may be written in Einstein variables as
\begin{equation}
1+z_{\rm obs}
= \frac{\nu_{\rm emit,J}}{\nu_{\rm obs,J}}
= \frac{\nu_{\rm emit,E}/\Apsi_{\rm emit}}{\nu_{\rm obs,E}/\Apsi_{\rm obs}}
= (1+z_{\rm E})\,\frac{\Apsi_{\rm obs}}{\Apsi_{\rm emit}},
\label{eq:zmap_endpoints_main}
\end{equation}
where \(1+z_{\rm E}\equiv \nu_{\rm emit,E}/\nu_{\rm obs,E}\) is the redshift defined in the Einstein
variables.

\paragraph{Homogeneous cancellation (why the background ladder is standard in Jordan).}
In homogeneous FLRW, the Jordan and Einstein scale factors are related by
\(\aE = \sqrt{\Fpsi}\,\aJ = \aJ/\Apsi\). Therefore
\begin{equation}
(1+z_{\rm E})\,\frac{\Apsi_{\rm obs}}{\Apsi_{\rm emit}}
=
\frac{\aE(\tE_{\rm obs})}{\aE(\tE_{\rm emit})}\,\frac{\Apsi_{\rm obs}}{\Apsi_{\rm emit}}
=
\frac{\aJ(\tJ_{\rm obs})}{\aJ(\tJ_{\rm emit})}
\equiv 1+z_{\rm cos},
\end{equation}
so that in the strictly homogeneous limit \(z_{\rm obs}=z_{\rm cos}\) and the standard ladder relations
are recovered in the physical Jordan frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:jordan_ladder}). In other words, the
\emph{background} effect of \(\Apsi\) is already encoded in \(\HJ(z)\) once one solves the Jordan-frame
scalar--tensor background.

\paragraph{Where an additional \(\Delta\ln(1+z)\) imprint can arise.}
Any extra mapping between an ``underlying'' cosmological redshift and the observed redshift must come
from \emph{inhomogeneous} structure (endpoint and integrated effects) or from the backreaction of the
homogeneous mode on \(\HJ(z)\). Writing
\(\psi=\bar\psi(\tJ)+\delta\psi(x)\) and \(\Cpsi=\bar{\C}(\tJ)+\delta\C(x)\), the observer-local imprint
tested by the LTA mapping corresponds to packaging the net perturbative contribution as
\begin{equation}
\ln\!\left(\frac{1+\zobs}{1+\zcos}\right)
\equiv I
\simeq
\left[\delta\C\right]_{\rm obs}-\left[\delta\C\right]_{\rm emit}
+\int_\gamma k^\mu \nabla^{\mathrm{J}}_\mu(\delta\C)\,d\lambda,
\label{eq:zmap_C_main}
\end{equation}
which motivates the purely phenomenological \(I(\chi)\) parameterization used in the SN+BAO pipeline
as a controlled observer-local limit (Sec.~\ref{sec:lta_mapping}).

\subsection{Why the correction is naturally written as an \emph{integral} in $\ln(1+z)$}
\label{sec:photon_transport_integrated}

Even though Eq.~\eqref{eq:zmap_C_main} may be written as an endpoint difference plus an integrated
term, it is mathematically (and physically) natural to represent the \emph{perturbative} chronometric
imprint as an \emph{accumulated} contribution to \(\ln(1+z)\).

Along a photon path \(\gamma\), the difference
\(\mathcal{C}_{\rm obs}-\mathcal{C}_{\rm emit}\) may be written as a line integral of the local
gradient:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_{\rm obs}-\mathcal{C}_{\rm emit}
=
\int_{\gamma} d\mathcal{C}
=
\int_{\gamma} (\nabla_\mu \mathcal{C})\,dx^\mu
=
\int_{\gamma} (k^\mu \nabla_\mu \mathcal{C})\,d\lambda,
\label{eq:C_line_integral}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda\) is any affine parameter along the null geodesic.

This motivates defining an effective \emph{local} fractional frequency-drift contribution along the
line of sight and integrating it in \(\ln(1+z)\), exactly as done in the LTA pipeline.
In the isotropic observer-local approximation used for SN+BAO fitting, we parameterize this imprint
as a function of comoving distance alone:
\begin{equation}
I(\chi) \;\equiv\; \mathcal{C}_{\rm obs}-\mathcal{C}_{\rm emit}(\chi),
\qquad
I(\chi)=\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{\chi} s(\chi')\,d\chi',
\qquad
s(\chi)\equiv c\,\frac{dI}{d\chi}.
\label{eq:I_s_def_main}
\end{equation}
Combining Eq.~\eqref{eq:z_cos_flrw} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_s_def_main} yields the LTA mapping used in
the likelihood:
\begin{equation}
1+z_{\rm obs} = (1+z_{\rm cos})\,\exp[I(\chi)].
\label{eq:zmap_main_rederived}
\end{equation}

A useful differential form makes explicit the “sum along the path” intuition:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{d\chi}\ln(1+z_{\rm obs})
=
\frac{d}{d\chi}\ln(1+z_{\rm cos}) + \frac{s(\chi)}{c}
=
\frac{H(z_{\rm cos})}{c} + \frac{s(\chi)}{c},
\label{eq:dln1pz_dchi}
\end{equation}
where we used \(dz_{\rm cos}/d\chi = H(z_{\rm cos})/c\) in FLRW.
Thus \(s(\chi)\) is precisely the additional local contribution to the \emph{log-redshift
accumulation} per unit comoving distance.

\subsection{Universal law vs.\ the observer-local limit tested in this paper}
\label{sec:photon_transport_universal_vs_local}

Conceptually, Eq.~\eqref{eq:clk_factor_main} is intended as a \emph{universal} law:
wherever sustained organization increases \(\psi\), the realized accumulation rate of clock time is
biased by \(A(\psi)\).
In a fully general cosmological setting, \(\psi(x)\) would be sourced by \emph{all} organized systems
(and potentially by organized eras) and may vary across space and time. The net imprint on an
incoming photon would then be described by the same local transport statement
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:C_line_integral}), with possible direction dependence:
\begin{equation}
I[\gamma] = \int_{\gamma} (k^\mu \nabla_\mu \mathcal{C})\,d\lambda.
\label{eq:I_general_path}
\end{equation}

However, the empirical anomaly we isolate in the SN Hubble diagram is sharply localized to the
nearby volume. For the present paper we therefore test a \emph{controlled observer-local dominant}
limit: we model the redshift-dependent part of \(I(\chi)\) as arising primarily from the evolution
and causal reach of the chronometric field sourced in the observer’s local environment. This
produces an approximately isotropic mapping at low redshift while remaining falsifiable through the
BAO Jacobian (Sec.~\ref{sec:bao_like}). Generalizing \(I[\gamma]\) beyond the observer-local limit
(i.e.\ including distributed cosmological sources of \(\psi\)) is an important next step, but it is
not required to test whether the \emph{local} Hubble-flow residual structure prefers an integrated
\(\Delta\ln(1+z)\) mapping.


\subsection{Jordan-frame scalar--tensor completion and a derived chronometric factor}
\label{sec:conformal_completion}

To make the chronometric hypothesis mathematically well-posed \emph{and} to ensure a self-consistent
late-time cosmology capable of mimicking dark-energy-like expansion histories, we adopt a standard
scalar--tensor (ST) structure and take the \emph{Jordan frame} to be the physical (matter) frame.
Operational observables---atomic clock rates, Cepheid periods, supernova light-curve timescales,
ruler lengths, and the redshift measured by spectroscopy---are defined with respect to the Jordan
metric $\gJ_{\mu\nu}$.

\paragraph{Jordan-frame action (physical matter frame).}
We work with signature $(-,+,+,+)$ and define $\Mp^{2}\equiv(8\pi G)^{-1}$.
A minimal ST completion with an additional nonequilibrium ``order'' sector is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}
=
\int d^4x\,\sqrt{-g_{\mathrm{J}}}\left[
\frac{\Mp^{2}}{2}\,\Fpsi\,R_{\mathrm{J}}
-\frac{f^{2}}{2}\,(\nabla_{\mathrm{J}}\psi)^2
-f^{4}\Upsi
\right]
+\mathcal{S}_{\rm m}[\,\gJ,\Phi\,]
+\mathcal{S}_{\rm neq}[\,\gJ,\psi,\Xi\,],
\label{eq:JF_action}
\end{equation}
where $F$ and $U$ are dimensionless functions, $f$ is a stiffness scale (mass dimension 1),
$(\nabla_{\mathrm{J}}\psi)^2\equiv \gJ^{\mu\nu}\nabla_\mu\psi\nabla_\nu\psi$, and
$\mathcal{S}_{\rm neq}$ is the covariant insertion point for sustained nonequilibrium activity /
constraint-maintenance that sources $\psi$.

\paragraph{Einstein frame and the \emph{derived} chronometric factor.}
Define the Einstein-frame metric by the standard conformal transformation
\begin{equation}
\gE_{\mu\nu} \equiv \Fpsi\, \gJ_{\mu\nu}.
\label{eq:conformal_J_to_E}
\end{equation}
In this frame the gravitational sector is canonical, but the matter action becomes conformally
coupled:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_{\rm m}[\,\gJ,\Phi\,]
=
\mathcal{S}_{\rm m}[\,\Apsi^2 \gE,\Phi\,],
\qquad
\Apsi \equiv \Fpsi^{-1/2},
\label{eq:A_derived}
\end{equation}
so the conformal/chronometric factor $\Apsi$ is \emph{not} an independent postulate in the scalar--tensor
completion; it is \emph{derived} from the Jordan-frame coupling function $F(\psi)$.

It is useful to define the chronometric potential
\begin{equation}
\Cpsi \equiv \ln \Apsi = -\frac{1}{2}\ln \Fpsi,
\label{eq:C_def}
\end{equation}
so that small variations are $\delta \Cpsi = -\frac{1}{2}\delta\ln F$.

\paragraph{Clock-time relation (frame conversion).}
Physical clock time is Jordan proper time $d\tau_{\mathrm{J}}$.
In Einstein-frame variables,
\begin{equation}
d\tau_{\mathrm{J}}^2
\equiv
-\gJ_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu
=
-\Apsi^{2}\,\gE_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu
\equiv
\Apsi^{2}\,d\tau_{\mathrm{E}}^2
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
d\tau_{\mathrm{J}} = \Apsi\, d\tau_{\mathrm{E}}.
\label{eq:clock_relation_JE}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:clock_relation_JE} is the precise sense in which a scalar--tensor completion
induces a ``chronometric factor'': it is the conversion between Einstein-frame proper time and the
proper time measured by matter clocks.

\paragraph{Homogeneous limit vs.\ observer-local imprints.}
In a homogeneous FLRW background, the measured cosmological redshift is determined by the Jordan
scale factor: $1+z = \aJ(t_0)/\aJ(t_e)$.
Section~\ref{sec:model} re-derives the SN/BAO distance ladder in the Jordan frame based on the
Jordan-frame expansion $\HJ(z)$ implied by \eqref{eq:JF_action}. Local time-acceleration effects
correspond to \emph{inhomogeneous} perturbations $\psi=\bar\psi(t)+\delta\psi(x,t)$, which can generate
endpoint and integrated contributions to the observed redshift and time-domain observables. The
phenomenological LTA mapping of earlier versions of this work is recovered as a controlled
observer-local limit (Sec.~\ref{sec:lta_mapping}).

\label{sec:model_hierarchy}

To keep the theory logically unified yet empirically testable, we distinguish three layers:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Jordan-frame scalar--tensor completion (theory level).}
We posit an action of the form \eqref{eq:JF_action} in which the order scalar \(\psi(x)\) enters the

\item \textbf{Homogeneous background (cosmology level).}

(Secs.~\ref{sec:jordan_background}--\ref{sec:jordan_ladder}). This is the channel through which the

\item \textbf{Observer-local imprint (controlled phenomenology).}
Inhomogeneities \(\delta\psi(x)\) and their associated chronometric potential perturbations

\(1+\zobs=(1+\zcos)\exp[I(\chi)]\) (Sec.~\ref{sec:lta_mapping}), which is a controlled approximation to
the inhomogeneous scalar--tensor propagation problem.
\end{enumerate}

This hierarchy clarifies what is and is not assumed in any given fit: the SN+BAO mapping layer tests
whether the data prefer an observer-local distortion, while the Jordan-frame background layer is the

\subsection{Effective sourced scalar dynamics}

\section{Thermodynamic foundation: from energy throughput to a bounded constraint inventory}
\label{sec:thermo_foundation}

The empirical LTA mapping in this paper is written as an accumulated contribution to
$\ln(1+z)$ along the past light cone. To connect that phenomenology to established physics, we need 
a quantitative, well-behaved scalar that captures ``order / constraint creation'' in a way that (i) 
is mathematically precise under coarse-graining, (ii) is directly tied to the second law, and (iii) 
admits hard bounds from energy throughput.

The key choice is to represent sustained ``constraint'' as \emph{relative entropy} (KL divergence)
with respect to an appropriate maximum-entropy (reference) distribution.
This is standard in nonequilibrium statistical mechanics and stochastic thermodynamics, where
relative entropy quantifies the \emph{excess} thermodynamic potential available in a nonequilibrium
state \cite{Seifert2012ST,Parrondo2015ThermoInfo}.

Throughout this section, we assume a coarse-grained description in which microstates are labeled
by $x$ and the system has an instantaneous state distribution $p(x,t)$. We also assume a chosen
reference distribution $\pi(\xi)$ representing the maximally unstructured (maximum-entropy) baseline
given the constraints one is \emph{not} counting as ``order'' (e.g.\ a canonical equilibrium, or a
MaxEnt state under specified macroscopic constraints).

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Lemma 1: A constraint inventory $\varphi$ that is nonnegative, monotone under coarse-graining, and additive under composition}
\label{sec:lemma1_constraint_inventory}

\paragraph{Definition (constraint inventory).}
Define the dimensionless \emph{constraint inventory}
\begin{equation}
\phi(t) \;\equiv\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(p(\cdot,t)\,\|\,\pi(\cdot)\bigr)
\;=\;
\int \! \dd x\; p(x,t)\,\ln\!\frac{p(x,t)}{\pi(\xi)},
\label{eq:phi_def}
\end{equation}
and its entropy-unit version
\begin{equation}
\varphi(t) \;\equiv\; k_B\,\phi(t)
\qquad [\varphi]=\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
\label{eq:varphi_def}
\end{equation}
The KL divergence was introduced in \cite{KullbackLeibler1951}.

We will refer to $\varphi$ as the ``constraint inventory'' because it measures how many
\emph{nats} (or, dividing by $\ln 2$, \emph{bits}) of information are required to specify the
additional structure in $p$ relative to the chosen reference $\pi$.

\paragraph{Lemma 1a (nonnegativity).}
\begin{equation}
\varphi(t)\ge 0 \quad \text{for all }t,\qquad
\varphi(t)=0 \iff p(\cdot,t)=\pi(\cdot)\;\text{a.e.}
\label{eq:phi_nonneg}
\end{equation}
\emph{Reason:} $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot\|\cdot)$ is nonnegative by Gibbs' inequality.

\paragraph{Lemma 1b (monotonicity under coarse-graining).}
Let $\mathcal{M}$ be any stochastic coarse-graining (Markov) map from microstates $x$ to
macro-cells $y$; denote $p_y=\mathcal{M}p$ and $\pi_y=\mathcal{M}\pi$. Then
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|\pi) \;\ge\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_y\|\pi_y),
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
\varphi \;\ge\; \varphi_y.
\label{eq:data_processing}
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{data-processing inequality} for KL divergence: coarse-graining cannot increase
distinguishability from the reference and therefore cannot increase the counted constraint
inventory \cite{CoverThomas2006}.

\paragraph{Lemma 1c (additivity / extensivity under independent composition).}
If the system decomposes into weakly interacting subsystems with
$p(x_1,x_2)=p_1(x_1)p_2(x_2)$ and $\pi(x_1,x_2)=\pi_1(x_1)\pi_2(x_2)$, then
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|\pi)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_1\|\pi_1)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_2\|\pi_2),
\qquad
\varphi=\varphi_1+\varphi_2.
\label{eq:additivity}
\end{equation}
Thus $\varphi$ is \emph{extensive} under composition when correlations between components are
negligible at the chosen coarse-graining scale.

\paragraph{Microstate-space interpretation (explicit link to ``constraining microstates'').}
In the special case where $\pi$ is uniform on an allowed set of size $\Omega_0$ and $p$ is uniform
on a constrained subset of size $\Omega_1\le \Omega_0$, one finds
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|\pi)=\ln\!\frac{\Omega_0}{\Omega_1},
\qquad
\varphi = k_B \ln\!\frac{\Omega_0}{\Omega_1}.
\label{eq:phi_microstates}
\end{equation}
So $\varphi$ literally counts the log-compression of accessible microstates induced by the
constraints being maintained.

\paragraph{Comment (why we do \emph{not} anchor on entropy alone).}
Total entropy production is a throughput and is always nonnegative, but it does not by itself
distinguish ``order creation'' from generic dissipation. By contrast, $\varphi$ is a \emph{state
variable} that measures how far the maintained state is from a maximum-entropy reference and thus
tracks constraint maintenance directly; the second law will then bound how fast $\varphi$ can grow.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Lemma 2: $T\,\varphi$ is the nonequilibrium free-energy budget stored in constraints}
\label{sec:lemma2_free_energy}

To connect $\varphi$ to energetics, specialize to a canonical reference distribution
\begin{equation}
\pi(\xi)=\frac{e^{-\beta E(x)}}{Z},\qquad \beta\equiv (k_B T)^{-1},
\label{eq:canonical_pi}
\end{equation}
at temperature $T$ for the same energy function $E(x)$.

Define the (instantaneous) nonequilibrium Helmholtz free energy functional
\begin{equation}
F[p] \;\equiv\; \langle E\rangle_p - T S[p],
\qquad
S[p]\equiv -k_B\int \dd x\, p(x)\ln p(x),
\label{eq:F_def}
\end{equation}
and the equilibrium free energy
\begin{equation}
F_{\rm eq}\equiv -k_B T \ln Z.
\label{eq:Feq_def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Lemma 2 (relative entropy identity).}
Under the canonical choice \eqref{eq:canonical_pi},
\begin{equation}
F[p] - F_{\rm eq} \;=\; k_B T\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|\pi)
\;=\; T\,\varphi.
\label{eq:F_minus_Feq}
\end{equation}
\emph{Proof sketch:} Insert $\ln\pi=-\beta E-\ln Z$ into the definition of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$, then
use $S[p]=-k_B\int p\ln p$ and $F_{\rm eq}=-k_B T\ln Z$.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:F_minus_Feq} states that $\varphi$ is not a metaphor: it is exactly the
\emph{excess free energy above equilibrium} stored in the maintained constraints. This is a standard 
result in nonequilibrium thermodynamics and stochastic thermodynamics \cite{Seifert2012ST,Parrondo2015ThermoInfo}.

\paragraph{Bits and Landauer scaling (optional but useful).}
Define $\varphi_{\rm bits}\equiv \varphi/(k_B\ln 2)=\phi/\ln 2$. Then $T\varphi$ corresponds to an
energy scale of $k_B T\ln 2$ per bit of maintained constraint, matching the Landauer cost scale for 
logically irreversible information erasure \cite{Landauer1961,Bennett1982}.
We emphasize: this does \emph{not} claim biology saturates Landauer bounds; it is a hard lower bound
and provides a clean dimensional bridge between constraint maintenance and energetic cost.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Lemma 3: The second law bounds $\dot\varphi$ by energy throughput}
\label{sec:lemma3_power_bound}

We now connect constraint growth to the second law in a form that yields a quantitative bound from
power flow.

\paragraph{Scope of the bound.}
The inequalities below are stated for an effective \emph{isothermal} driven setting at a single temperature \(T\),
with a fixed reference distribution \(\pi\) (equivalently fixed \(F_{\rm eq}\)) for the degrees of freedom whose
constraint inventory is being tracked. In more general settings with multiple reservoirs, chemical work, or
time-dependent reference constraints, the same structure holds with \(P_{\rm in}\) interpreted as the appropriate
generalized free-energy/work influx, but we restrict to the isothermal form here because it is sufficient for
the conservative Earth-budget envelope used later.


Consider a system coupled to an (effective) isothermal reservoir at temperature $T$.
Let $\dot Q$ be heat flow \emph{into} the system and $\dot W$ be work done \emph{on} the system.
The first law reads
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\langle E\rangle = \dot Q + \dot W.
\label{eq:first_law_rates}
\end{equation}
Define total entropy production rate (system + environment) as
\begin{equation}
\dot\Sigma \;\equiv\; \frac{\dd S}{\dd t} - \frac{\dot Q}{T}\;\ge\;0,
\label{eq:entropy_prod_rate}
\end{equation}
where the inequality is the (local) second law for this driven isothermal setting \cite{Seifert2012ST}.

Using $F=\langle E\rangle-TS$ and Eqs.~\eqref{eq:first_law_rates}--\eqref{eq:entropy_prod_rate},
one obtains the standard work--free-energy--dissipation identity:
\begin{equation}
\dot W \;=\; \frac{\dd F}{\dd t} + T\,\dot\Sigma.
\label{eq:work_balance}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Lemma 3a (power bounds constraint growth).}
If the reference equilibrium $F_{\rm eq}$ is fixed (same $E(x)$ and $T$), then Lemma~2 implies
$\dd(F-F_{\rm eq})/\dd t = T\,\dot\varphi$. Combining with \eqref{eq:work_balance} and $\dot\Sigma\ge0$ gives
\begin{equation}
T\,\dot\varphi \;\le\; \dot W \equiv P_{\rm in},
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
\dot\varphi \;\le\; \frac{P_{\rm in}}{T}.
\label{eq:varphi_dot_bound}
\end{equation}
This is the rigorous form of the chain
\[
\text{energy consumption} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{bounded constraint accumulation}.
\]

\paragraph{Lemma 3b (relation to entropy production).}

\subsection{Lemma 4 (Postulate): a chronometric coupling that biases clock rates without modifying spacetime geometry}
\label{sec:lemma4_chronometric}

Lemmas~\ref{sec:lemma1_constraint_inventory}--\ref{sec:lemma3_power_bound} provide a standard,

\begin{equation}
d\tau_{\rm clk} = A(\psi)\,d\tau,
\qquad
A(0)=1,
\qquad
\frac{\partial A}{\partial \psi} > 0.
\label{eq:clock_factor_postulate}
\end{equation}
Thus sustained organization ($\psi>0$) increases the local accumulation rate of clock time relative
to GR proper time (``local time acceleration'').

For small $\psi$ we parameterize

A(\psi)=\exp(\epsilon\,\psi)\simeq 1+\epsilon\,\psi+\mathcal{O}(\psi^2),
\label{eq:A_of_psi}
\end{equation}
with a dimensionless coupling $\epsilon$.

\paragraph{Frequency measurement and redshift mapping.}

\(A(\psi_{\rm obs})/A(\psi_{\rm emit})\). The full derivation (including the integrated \(\Delta\ln(1+z)\) form,
its local drift representation, and the general path-dependent expression \(I[\gamma]\)) is given in
Sec.~\ref{sec:photon_transport}. Here we record only the resulting mapping used in the SN+BAO likelihood:
\begin{equation}
1+z_{\rm obs} = (1+z_{\rm cos})\,\exp[I],
\qquad
I\equiv \ln\!\frac{A(\psi_{\rm obs})}{A(\psi_{\rm emit})}.

\paragraph{Causality in the observer-local dominant limit.}
In the low-$z$ anomaly tested here, the redshift-dependent part of \(I\) is modeled as being dominated by the
observer’s local environment and its causal reach; this motivates evaluating the activation through an
observer-retarded time argument \(t_{\rm ret}(\chi)\) (Sec.~\ref{sec:psi_dynamics_main} and Sec.~\ref{sec:tret2}).


\paragraph{Relation to the phenomenological drift field $s(\chi)$.}

main analysis:
\begin{equation}
s(\chi) \equiv c\,\frac{dI}{d\chi}.
\label{eq:s_def_chrono}
\end{equation}
With $A(\psi)=\exp(\epsilon\psi)$ this becomes
\begin{equation}
s(\chi)= -c\,\epsilon\,\frac{d\psi_{\rm emit}(\chi)}{d\chi},
\label{eq:s_chrono_psi}
\end{equation}
where the minus sign reflects that more distant emission events typically correspond to earlier
(retarded) epochs when $\psi$ was smaller.
Under the simple causal bookkeeping used in this work, $t_{\rm ret}\approx 2\chi/c$ at low redshift,
so $d\psi_{\rm emit}/d\chi \approx (2/c)\,\dot\psi(t_{\rm ret})$, giving the useful approximate link
\begin{equation}
s(\chi)\approx -2\epsilon\,\dot\psi\!\bigl(t_{\rm ret}(\chi)\bigr)
\qquad (\text{low-$z$ causal approximation}).
\label{eq:s_approx_psi_dot}

This connects the empirical $s(t_{\rm ret})$ profile directly to the time-derivative of the
underlying order field.

\paragraph{No new lensing by construction.}

trajectories (and therefore gravitational lensing/deflection predictions) remain those of the

Using \eqref{eq:s_approx_psi_dot}, this implies an energetics-based envelope on the LTA drift field,

\label{eq:s_energy_bound_chrono}

and correspondingly a bound on the saturation imprint $I_{\rm sat}=\max I(\chi)$ in terms of the
integrated Earth-system energy/entropy throughput over the active epoch.

\section{Model definition: Jordan-frame scalar--tensor background plus local chronometric imprint}
\label{sec:model}

This section specifies the forward model used throughout in a form suitable for a

\item An \textbf{optional local chronometric imprint} (observer-local perturbation) that can shift

\subsection{Jordan-frame background: expansion history from scalar--tensor dynamics}
\label{sec:jordan_background}

\paragraph{Background geometry and redshift.}
We take the physical (matter) metric to be a spatially flat FLRW line element in the Jordan frame:
\begin{equation}
ds_{\mathrm{J}}^{2} = -d\tJ^{2} + \aJ^{2}(\tJ)\,d\bm{x}^{2},
\qquad
\HJ \equiv \frac{1}{\aJ}\frac{d\aJ}{d\tJ}.
\end{equation}
The \emph{cosmological} redshift is defined by
\begin{equation}
1+\zcos \equiv \frac{\aJ(\tJ_0)}{\aJ(\tJ_e)}.
\label{eq:zcos_J_def}
\end{equation}
In the homogeneous limit, the observed redshift equals the cosmological redshift:
$\zobs=\zcos$ up to the standard local corrections (peculiar velocities, local gravitational potentials).

\paragraph{Field equations (Jordan frame).}
Varying the Jordan-frame action \eqref{eq:JF_action} yields
\begin{equation}
\Mp^{2}\Fpsi\,G^{\mathrm{J}}_{\mu\nu}
=
T^{(m)}_{\mu\nu}
+
T^{(\psi)}_{\mu\nu}
+
T^{(\rm neq)}_{\mu\nu}
+
\Mp^{2}\left(\nabla^{\mathrm{J}}_{\mu}\nabla^{\mathrm{J}}_{\nu}\Fpsi - \gJ_{\mu\nu}\Box_{\mathrm{J}}\Fpsi\right),
\label{eq:JF_einstein}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
T^{(\psi)}_{\mu\nu}
=
f^{2}\nabla^{\mathrm{J}}_{\mu}\psi\,\nabla^{\mathrm{J}}_{\nu}\psi
-
\gJ_{\mu\nu}\left[\frac{f^{2}}{2}(\nabla_{\mathrm{J}}\psi)^2+f^{4}\Upsi\right],
\label{eq:JF_Tpsi}
\end{equation}
and a scalar equation
\begin{equation}
f^{2}\Box_{\mathrm{J}}\psi - f^{4}\Upsi' + \frac{\Mp^{2}}{2}\Fpsi'\,R_{\mathrm{J}}
=
J_{\rm neq},
\qquad
J_{\rm neq}\equiv -\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g_{\mathrm{J}}}}\frac{\delta \mathcal{S}_{\rm neq}}{\delta\psi}.
\label{eq:JF_scalar}
\end{equation}
In this Jordan-first setup, matter is minimally coupled, so $T^{(m)}_{\mu\nu}$ is covariantly conserved
with respect to $\nabla^{\mathrm{J}}$ (in the absence of explicit matter--$\Xi$ exchange in $\mathcal{S}_{\rm neq}$).

\paragraph{Friedmann form.}
For a homogeneous $\psi(\tJ)$, the $00$ equation may be written as
\begin{equation}
3\Mp^{2}\Fpsi\,\HJ^{2}
=
\rho_m+\rho_r+\rho_\psi+\rho_{\rm neq}
-3\Mp^{2}\HJ\,\frac{d\Fpsi}{d\tJ},
\label{eq:JF_friedmann}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\rho_\psi = \frac{f^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{d\psi}{d\tJ}\right)^{2} + f^{4}\Upsi,
\end{equation}
and $\rho_{\rm neq}$ denotes the homogeneous energy density carried by the nonequilibrium sector
(if present in the background). The corresponding acceleration equation can be written as
\begin{equation}
-2\Mp^{2}\Fpsi\,\frac{d\HJ}{d\tJ}
=
\left(\rho_m+p_m\right)+\left(\rho_r+p_r\right)+\left(\rho_\psi+p_\psi\right)
+\left(\rho_{\rm neq}+p_{\rm neq}\right)
+\Mp^{2}\left(\frac{d^{2}\Fpsi}{d\tJ^{2}}-\HJ\,\frac{d\Fpsi}{d\tJ}\right),
\label{eq:JF_accel}
\end{equation}
with $p_m\simeq 0$, $p_r=\rho_r/3$, and
\begin{equation}
p_\psi = \frac{f^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{d\psi}{d\tJ}\right)^{2} - f^{4}\Upsi.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Practical parameterization.}
For inference, we continue to parameterize the late-time background by a present-day Hubble scale $H_0$
and a present-day matter density fraction $\Om$ (defined in the Jordan frame), together with scalar-sector
parameters specifying $\Fpsi$ and $\Upsi$ (and any nonequilibrium/source parameters in $\mathcal{S}_{\rm neq}$).
In general there is no closed-form $E(z)$; instead $\HJ(z)$ is obtained by solving the coupled background system.

\subsection{Observable ladder in the homogeneous Jordan background}
\label{sec:jordan_ladder}

Given $\HJ(z)$, the distance ladder follows the standard FLRW relations \emph{in the Jordan frame}
(where matter defines clocks and rulers).

\paragraph{Comoving distance and lookback time.}
The line-of-sight comoving distance is
\begin{equation}
\chis(z) = c\int_0^{z}\frac{\dd z'}{\HJ(z')},
\label{eq:chi_def}
\end{equation}
and the proper lookback time is
\begin{equation}
\tlb(z) = \int_0^{z}\frac{\dd z'}{(1+z')\HJ(z')}.
\label{eq:tlb_def}
\end{equation}
(These reduce to the familiar $\Lambda$CDM expressions when the model reduces to GR+$\Lambda$.)

\paragraph{Luminosity and angular-diameter distances.}
For spatial flatness,
\begin{equation}
D_M(z)=\chis(z),
\qquad
D_A(z)=\frac{\chis(z)}{1+z},
\qquad
d_L(z)=(1+z)\chis(z).
\label{eq:distances_J}
\end{equation}
The SN distance modulus prediction is therefore
\begin{equation}
\mu_{\mathrm{pred}}(z) = 5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d_L(z)}{\Mpc}\right)+25.
\label{eq:mu_pred_J}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{BAO observables.}
The standard BAO distance combinations in the Jordan background are
\begin{equation}
D_H(z)\equiv \frac{c}{\HJ(z)},
\qquad
D_V(z)\equiv \left[z\,D_H(z)\,D_M^2(z)\right]^{1/3},
\label{eq:bao_defs_J}
\end{equation}
and the comparison to data is made through ratios to the sound horizon $r_d$.
In this paper we retain a phenomenological scaling parameter $\alpha_{\rm rd}\equiv r_d/r_{d,\rm fid}$
in the BAO likelihood; in the fully self-consistent program, $r_d$ is computed from the
Jordan-frame early-time dynamics.

\subsection{Local chronometric imprint and connection to the LTA mapping}
\label{sec:lta_mapping}

In a strictly homogeneous Jordan-frame cosmology, $\zobs=\zcos$ and the ladder above is complete.
Observer-local time-acceleration effects correspond to \emph{inhomogeneous} chronometric structure
(e.g.\ $\psi=\bar\psi(t)+\delta\psi(x,t)$) that modifies clock comparisons and therefore the
operationally inferred redshift.

A convenient way to connect this local-perturbation picture back to the phenomenological LTA
analysis is to package the net chronometric imprint into an additive contribution to $\ln(1+z)$:
\begin{equation}
1+\zobs = (1+\zcos)\exp\!\bigl[\Iofchi(\zcos)\bigr].
\label{eq:zmap2}
\end{equation}
Here $\zcos$ is the cosmological redshift defined by the homogeneous Jordan background
\eqref{eq:zcos_J_def}, while $\zobs$ is the measured redshift including the observer-local imprint.

As in the phenomenological formulation, we define
\begin{equation}
\Iofchi \equiv \frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{\chis} \sofchi' \,\dd \chis',
\label{eq:I_def2}
\end{equation}
where $\sofchi$ has dimensions of a Hubble rate (e.g.\ $\kmsMpc$) and $\sofchi/c$ plays the role of an
effective fractional frequency drift per unit comoving distance in the observer-local approximation.

Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:zmap2} and using $\dd\chis/\dd\zcos = c/\HJ(\zcos)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \Iofchi}{\dd \zcos}
= \frac{1}{c}\sofchi(\zcos)\frac{\dd \chis}{\dd \zcos}
= \frac{\sofchi(\zcos)}{\HJ(\zcos)}.
\label{eq:dIdz}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Earth-retarded lookback time construction}
\label{sec:tret2}

To encode the motivating ``observer-sourced'' (Earth-anchored) character in a causally consistent
way, we evaluate the activation along the photon path using an \emph{Earth-retarded lookback time}
argument $\tret(\chis)$ computed in the same Jordan-frame background.

For a given line-of-sight distance $\chis$ (corresponding to $\zcos$), we define an ``Earth emission''
redshift $z_e$ implicitly by
\begin{equation}
\chis(z_e) = 2\chis,
\label{eq:ze_def}
\end{equation}
and then set
\begin{equation}
\tret(\chis) \equiv \tlb(z_e).
\label{eq:tret_def}
\end{equation}
At low redshift, where $\chis\approx (c/H_0)z$ and $\tlb\approx H_0^{-1}z$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ze_def}
implies $z_e\approx 2z$ and $\tret\approx 2\tlb$, making the causal bookkeeping interpretation explicit.

\subsection{Bounded activation and parameterization of $\sofchi$}
\label{sec:earth_history2}

We parameterize the drift-rate profile as a bounded activation scaled by an amplitude defined at an
explicit \emph{anchor time} $\tA$:
\begin{equation}
\sofchi = s_{\mathrm{anchor}}\,
\frac{g\!\bigl(\tret(\chis)\bigr)}{g(\tA)}.
\label{eq:s_profile2}
\end{equation}
Here $g(t)\in[0,1]$ is an activation function of Earth-retarded lookback time, normalized so that
$g(0)=1$ and $g(t)=0$ for $t\ge \tL$, where $\tL$ is a finite cutoff epoch.

\paragraph{Anchor-time choice (leakage control).}
To avoid any dependence of the LTA normalization on the calibrator subset, we compute $\tA$ using
only the Hubble-flow subset (excluding calibrators). Concretely, we define $\tA$ as the 5th
percentile of the $\tret$ distribution over Hubble-flow objects. This rule is stable under
resampling and keeps the normalization tied to the Hubble-flow regime that drives the cosmological fit.

\paragraph{Power-law activation family (fiducial choice).}
In this work we adopt a smooth power-law decay. For $0\le t\le \tL$ define
\begin{equation}
r(t) = \left(1+\frac{t}{B}\right)^{-p},
\end{equation}
with timescale $B>0$ (in $\mathrm{Gyr}$) and slope $p\ge 0$, and set
\begin{equation}
g(t)=\frac{r(t)-r(\tL)}{1-r(\tL)}.
\label{eq:g_powerlaw2}
\end{equation}
This guarantees $g(0)=1$ and $g(\tL)=0$, yielding a continuous, bounded activation with a tunable
early-time scale ($B$) and tail steepness ($p$).

\paragraph{Fiducial degrees of freedom (what is actually fit).}
Because the observer-local imprint enters through a line-of-sight integral, there are strong
degeneracies between overall amplitude and detailed curve-shape parameters. Our primary analysis
therefore fixes the activation \emph{shape} to representative smooth values and fits only the
amplitude $s_{\rm anchor}$ as the additional imprint degree of freedom beyond the background
parameters (including $\alpha_{\rm rd}$ in the BAO likelihood).

\paragraph{Optional modulation exponents (fixed in this work).}
The analysis code supports additional multiplicative modulation exponents (e.g.\ ``complexity'' and
``life'' weights) that rescale the activation family. In the fiducial results emphasized in this
paper, these exponents are fixed to unity so that they do not contribute additional degrees of freedom.

\subsection{Inverse mapping and redshift Jacobian}
\label{sec:inverse2}

Data are indexed by $\zobs$ (or by a measured redshift proxy such as $\zhd$ for SNe), whereas the
homogeneous background distances are naturally functions of $\zcos$.
Therefore, for each observed redshift $\zobs$ we obtain $\zcos(\zobs)$ by numerically inverting
Eq.~\eqref{eq:zmap2} under the monotonicity condition
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \zobs}{\dd \zcos} > 0.
\end{equation}
Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:zmap2} gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \zobs}{\dd \zcos}
= \exp\!\bigl[\Iofchi\bigr]
\left[
1+(1+\zcos)\frac{\dd \Iofchi}{\dd \zcos}
\right]
= \exp\!\bigl[\Iofchi\bigr]
\left[
1+(1+\zcos)\frac{\sofchi(\zcos)}{\HJ(\zcos)}
\right],
\label{eq:jac_general2}
\end{equation}
where the final equality uses Eq.~\eqref{eq:dIdz}. This Jacobian enters directly in radial BAO
predictions and serves as an important diagnostic of mapping regularity.

\subsection{Distances and effective expansion observables as functions of $\zobs$}
\label{sec:obsfuncs}

The comoving distance as a function of observed redshift is evaluated as
\begin{equation}
\chis(\zobs) \equiv \chis\!\bigl(\zcos(\zobs)\bigr),
\end{equation}
with $\chis(z)$ computed in the Jordan background via Eq.~\eqref{eq:chi_def}. For radial derivatives,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \chis}{\dd \zobs}
= \frac{\dd \chis}{\dd \zcos}\frac{\dd \zcos}{\dd \zobs}
= \frac{c}{\HJ(\zcos)}\left(\frac{\dd \zobs}{\dd \zcos}\right)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the \emph{effective} Hubble rate inferred from the $\zobs$--$\chis$ relation is
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\zobs)\equiv \frac{c}{\dd \chis/\dd \zobs}
= \HJ(\zcos)\left(\frac{\dd \zobs}{\dd \zcos}\right).
\label{eq:Heff_def}
\end{equation}
Here ``effective'' means ``as inferred from the $\zobs$--distance mapping in the presence of an
observer-local chronometric imprint''; it does \emph{not} redefine the homogeneous background law $\HJ(z)$.

In the joint SN+BAO analysis below, transverse distances depend primarily on $\chis(\zobs)$, while
radial observables depend explicitly on the Jacobian factor $\dd \zobs/\dd \zcos$ through
$H_{\rm eff}(\zobs)$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:bao_like}).

\section{Consistency constraints and falsification channels}
\label{sec:constraints_main}

A Jordan-frame scalar--tensor completion has sharp falsification channels in:
(i) local gravity tests (PPN, fifth forces, and time variation of the effective Newton constant),
(ii) clock and redshift comparisons (as operational probes of inhomogeneous endpoint/integrated terms),
and (iii) early-universe calibration (sound horizon, recombination, and CMB inference).
This section records the principal channels that must be confronted as the analysis moves from the
observer-local mapping limit to a fully self-consistent cosmology.

\subsection{Local gravity constraints: PPN, fifth forces, and $\dot G_{\rm eff}/G_{\rm eff}$}
\label{sec:constraints_clocks}

In the Jordan-first completion, matter is minimally coupled in the physical frame, while the
effective gravitational coupling scales as
\begin{equation}
G_{\rm eff} \propto \frac{1}{F(\psi)}.
\end{equation}
Therefore any late-time evolution of \(\bar\psi(t)\) generically implies a time variation of
\(G_{\rm eff}\) and potentially fifth-force/PPN signatures unless the model includes a screening
mechanism or a parameter regime compatible with local tests.

In addition to cosmological fits, any viable parameter region must satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Bounds on \(\dot G/G\)} from solar-system, binary pulsar, and other timing constraints,
interpretable as \(\dot F/F\) bounds in the model.
\item \textbf{PPN constraints / equivalence principle tests} that limit scalar-mediated deviations
from GR in weak-field environments (see, e.g., \cite{Will2014LRR} and modern satellite redshift tests
\cite{Delva2018Galileo,Herrmann2018Galileo}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Time-domain consistency: spectroscopy vs.\ time dilation in transients}
\label{sec:constraints_timedomain}

A particularly crisp self-consistency check is that the redshift inferred from spectroscopy and the
time-dilation inferred from light-curve stretching (or other transient timescales) must agree once
all observables are computed in the same physical frame.

In a homogeneous Jordan-frame FLRW cosmology,
\begin{equation}
\Delta t_{\rm obs} = (1+z)\,\Delta t_{\rm emit},
\end{equation}
with the same \(z\) as inferred from spectral line ratios. In the presence of an observer-local
inhomogeneous imprint (the \(I(\chi)\) mapping limit), the theory must specify whether the imprint
acts as a purely spectroscopic endpoint/integrated frequency bias, a genuine modification of
propagation, or a combination. This is therefore a primary falsification channel for any model that
attempts to explain substantial cosmological effects via chronometric structure.

\subsection{Early-universe calibration: sound horizon, recombination, and Planck priors}
\label{sec:constraints_cmb}

Once the scalar--tensor completion is treated as the cosmological background (rather than a local
mapping), the sound horizon \(r_d\) and recombination-era observables are no longer external inputs:
they are \emph{model outputs} that depend on the physical expansion history \(\HJ(z)\) and on any
energy exchange implied by the Einstein-frame representation of the theory. Accordingly:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item The BAO ruler calibration (\(\alpha_{\rm rd}\) or \(r_d\)) should ultimately be computed from the
model, not treated purely phenomenologically.
\item Any use of Planck-derived priors extracted under \(\Lambda\)CDM must be treated as \emph{approximate}
until recombination and CMB inference are recomputed within the scalar--tensor model.
\end{enumerate}

For completeness we provide in Appendix~\ref{app:early_universe_full} the frame bookkeeping and
equations needed for the early-time extension; the current SN+BAO analysis retains a phenomenological
\(\alpha_{\rm rd}\) while the full early-universe closure is implemented.

\alpha_{\mathrm{rd}} \equiv \frac{r_d}{r_{d,\mathrm{fid}}}.
\end{equation}
To match the convention used in our code, the BAO observables are taken in the rescaled form
\begin{equation}
D_M^{(\mathrm{data})}(z) \equiv \left(\frac{D_M(z)}{r_d}\right) r_{d,\mathrm{fid}},
\qquad
H^{(\mathrm{data})}(z) \equiv \left(H(z)\,r_d\right)\frac{1}{r_{d,\mathrm{fid}}},
\end{equation}
so that the theory predictions become simple multiplicative scalings by $\alpha_{\mathrm{rd}}$ (see Sec.~\ref{sec:bao_like}).
We assume the BAO and SN datasets are independent so that the joint likelihood covariance is block diagonal between SN and BAO.

\section{Likelihood and inference}
\label{sec:likelihood}

\subsection{Supernova distance model and residual vector}
\label{sec:sn_model}

For each SN, we compute the luminosity distance using the comoving distance evaluated at the cosmological redshift corresponding to the \emph{observed} Hubble-diagram redshift:
\begin{equation}
d_L = (1+\zhel)\,\chis\!\bigl(\zcos(\zhd)\bigr),
\qquad
\mu_{\mathrm{pred}} = 5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d_L}{\Mpc}\right) + 25.
\label{eq:mu_pred}
\end{equation}
The corrected magnitude observable is modeled as
\begin{equation}
m_{b,i}^{\mathrm{corr}} = \mu_{\mathrm{pred}}(z_i;\bm{\theta}) + M + \epsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $M$ is an intercept (absolute magnitude) and $\epsilon$ is a correlated Gaussian error with the released covariance.

Let $\bm{y}$ be the full SN data vector (stacked $m_b^{\mathrm{corr}}$) and $\bm{\mu}(\bm{\theta})$ the corresponding model vector (stacked $\mu_{\mathrm{pred}}$). The raw residual vector at fixed $M$ is
\begin{equation}
\bm{r}(\bm{\theta},M) \equiv \bm{y} - \bm{\mu}(\bm{\theta}) - M\bm{1}.
\label{eq:r_full}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Ladder-anchored SN likelihood with calibrators and full cross-covariance}
\label{sec:sn_anchor_like}

A key feature of the Pantheon+SH0ES release is that calibrators and Hubble-flow objects are not independent: the covariance includes nontrivial cross-blocks. We therefore construct an \emph{anchored} likelihood that (i) retains the full covariance structure, (ii) uses calibrators as an absolute-distance anchor, and (iii) yields a Hubble-flow likelihood that can be evaluated repeatedly during cosmological optimization.

\paragraph{Calibrator / Hubble-flow partition.}
We partition the SN vector into calibrators (C) and Hubble-flow objects (H):
\begin{equation}
\bm{y}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{y}_C\\
\bm{y}_H
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\bm{\mu}(\bm{\theta})=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{\mu}_C\\
\bm{\mu}_H(\bm{\theta})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\bm{C}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{C}_{CC} & \bm{C}_{CH}\\
\bm{C}_{HC} & \bm{C}_{HH}
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For calibrators we take $\bm{\mu}_C$ from the external distance ladder (e.g.\ Cepheid-based distance moduli provided by the release), so $\bm{\mu}_C$ is fixed across cosmological models.

\paragraph{Step 1: calibrator-only posterior for $M$.}
Using calibrators alone, we compute the Gaussian posterior for the intercept:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_M^2 = \left(\bm{1}_C^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{C}_{CC}^{-1}\bm{1}_C\right)^{-1},
\qquad
M_{\mathrm{cal}} = \sigma_M^2\,\bm{1}_C^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{C}_{CC}^{-1}\bigl(\bm{y}_C-\bm{\mu}_C\bigr).
\label{eq:Mcal}
\end{equation}
In the representative run reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}, this yields
\begin{equation}
M_{\mathrm{cal}} = -19.248885 \pm 0.030106 \qquad (1\sigma,\ \text{calibrators only}).
\end{equation}
This posterior is then used as an informative Gaussian prior on $M$ when evaluating the Hubble-flow likelihood.

\paragraph{Step 2: condition Hubble-flow on calibrators (Schur complement).}
Define the cross-block regression operator
\begin{equation}
\bm{K}\equiv \bm{C}_{HC}\bm{C}_{CC}^{-1},
\end{equation}
and the conditional covariance (Schur complement)
\begin{equation}
\bm{S}\equiv \bm{C}_{HH}-\bm{C}_{HC}\bm{C}_{CC}^{-1}\bm{C}_{CH}.
\label{eq:schur}
\end{equation}
The Hubble-flow data conditioned on calibrators is represented by the shifted residual vector
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\bm{d}}(\bm{\theta}) \equiv
\bigl(\bm{y}_H-\bm{\mu}_H(\bm{\theta})\bigr)
-\bm{K}\bigl(\bm{y}_C-\bm{\mu}_C\bigr).
\label{eq:dtilde}
\end{equation}
At fixed intercept $M$, the conditional model is linear in $M$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\bm{d}}(\bm{\theta}) = \bm{a}\,M + \bm{\eta},
\qquad
\bm{a}\equiv \bm{1}_H-\bm{K}\bm{1}_C,
\qquad
\bm{\eta}\sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{S}).
\label{eq:cond_linear}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Step 3: marginalize $M$ using the calibrator posterior.}
We combine the conditional Gaussian likelihood in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cond_linear} with the calibrator posterior $M\sim\mathcal{N}(M_{\mathrm{cal}},\sigma_M^2)$ and analytically integrate $M$.
Up to additive constants independent of $\bm{\theta}$, this produces an effective Hubble-flow negative log-likelihood (written as a $\chi^2$ contribution):
\begin{equation}
\chi^2_{\mathrm{HF}}(\bm{\theta}) \equiv
\tilde{\bm{d}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{d}}
-\frac{\left(\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{d}} + M_{\mathrm{cal}}/\sigma_M^2\right)^2}
{\bm{a}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{a} + 1/\sigma_M^2}.
\label{eq:chi2_hf}
\end{equation}
The calibrator term itself contributes a constant (model-independent) offset once $M_{\mathrm{cal}}$ and $\sigma_M$ are fixed, so the cosmology-sensitive SN contribution in the optimizer is $\chi^2_{\mathrm{HF}}(\bm{\theta})$.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The SN objective used in fitting can be viewed as
\begin{equation}
\chi^2_{\mathrm{SN}}(\bm{\theta}) = \chi^2_{\mathrm{cal,const}} + \chi^2_{\mathrm{HF}}(\bm{\theta}),
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2_{\mathrm{cal,const}}$ is constant across models, and $\chi^2_{\mathrm{HF}}(\bm{\theta})$ incorporates both the calibrator anchor and the full cross-covariance via the Schur complement.

\subsection{BAO likelihood with LTA Jacobian}
\label{sec:bao_like}

Because BAO measurements are indexed by observed redshift $\zobs$ while comoving distances are functions of $\zcos$, the LTA mapping affects both transverse and radial predictions.

\paragraph{Transverse BAO.}
Using the rescaled data convention described in Sec.~\ref{sec:data_bao}, the model prediction is
\begin{equation}
D_M^{(\mathrm{model})}(\zobs) =
\frac{\chis\!\bigl(\zcos(\zobs)\bigr)}{\alpha_{\mathrm{rd}}}.
\label{eq:bao_DM_model}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Radial BAO.}
Radial BAO depends on $\dd\chis/\dd z$ and therefore requires the redshift Jacobian. Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:Heff_def}, the effective Hubble rate at observed redshift is
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\zobs) =
\Hc\!\bigl(\zcos(\zobs)\bigr)\left(\frac{\dd \zobs}{\dd \zcos}\right),
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
H^{(\mathrm{model})}(\zobs) = H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\zobs)\,\alpha_{\mathrm{rd}}
= \Hc\!\bigl(\zcos(\zobs)\bigr)\left(\frac{\dd \zobs}{\dd \zcos}\right)\alpha_{\mathrm{rd}},
\label{eq:bao_H_model}
\end{equation}
where $\dd \zobs/\dd \zcos$ is given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:jac_general2}.

\paragraph{BAO $\chi^2$.}
Let $\bm{y}_{\mathrm{BAO}}$ be the stacked BAO data vector and $\bm{m}_{\mathrm{BAO}}(\bm{\theta})$ the stacked model vector obtained from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:bao_DM_model}--\eqref{eq:bao_H_model}. With BAO covariance $\bm{C}_{\mathrm{BAO}}$, the contribution is
\begin{equation}
\chi^2_{\mathrm{BAO}}(\bm{\theta}) =
\bigl(\bm{y}_{\mathrm{BAO}}-\bm{m}_{\mathrm{BAO}}(\bm{\theta})\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}
\bm{C}_{\mathrm{BAO}}^{-1}
\bigl(\bm{y}_{\mathrm{BAO}}-\bm{m}_{\mathrm{BAO}}(\bm{\theta})\bigr).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Correlated Planck-chain Gaussian prior (optional; model-dependent)}
\label{sec:planck_prior}

Optionally, we include a correlated Gaussian prior derived from a Planck-chain subset.
Because Planck posteriors are model-dependent (typically extracted under \(\Lambda\)CDM and a specific
gravity sector), this prior is used only as an \emph{approximate} early-universe calibration aid
unless the full recombination/CMB inference is recomputed within the scalar--tensor model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:constraints_cmb} and App.~\ref{app:early_universe_full}).

In the code implementation, we support priors on either:
\begin{equation}
\bm{\phi} \equiv (\omega_m,\alpha_{\mathrm{rd}}),
\qquad
\text{or}\qquad
\bm{\phi}\equiv(H_0,\Omega_m,\alpha_{\mathrm{rd}}),
\end{equation}
where \(\omega_m\equiv \Omega_m h^2\) and \(\alpha_{\rm rd}\equiv r_d/r_{d,\rm fid}\).
The Gaussian prior takes the form
\begin{equation}
\chi^2_{\mathrm{prior}}(\bm{\phi})
= (\bm{\phi}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{P}})^{\mathsf{T}}
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{P}}^{-1}
(\bm{\phi}-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{P}}),
\end{equation}
with \(\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{P}}\) and \(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{P}}\) obtained from a chosen Planck chain subset.
When shown, the exact subset and parameter space are explicitly reported alongside fit results.

\subsection{Total objective and parameter sets}
\label{sec:params2}

The total objective minimized is
\begin{equation}
\chi^2_{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\theta}) =
\chi^2_{\mathrm{SN}}(\bm{\theta}) + \chi^2_{\mathrm{BAO}}(\bm{\theta}) + \chi^2_{\mathrm{prior}}(\bm{\phi}),
\label{eq:chi2_tot}
\end{equation}
where \(\chi^2_{\rm prior}\) is included only when explicitly enabled (Sec.~\ref{sec:planck_prior}).

\paragraph{Background parameter sets.}
We distinguish two background choices:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Model A (fixed background; legacy baseline):} a conventional flat \(\Lambda\)CDM background
used as a control case for the observer-local mapping limit.
\item \textbf{Model B (Jordan-frame scalar--tensor background):} a \(\Lambda=0\) scalar--tensor background
defined by \(\Fpsi\), \(\Upsi\), and any nonequilibrium/source parameters entering \(J_{\rm neq}\)
(Eqs.~\eqref{eq:JF_action}--\eqref{eq:JF_scalar}), from which \(\HJ(z)\) and the ladder are computed.
\end{enumerate}

Accordingly, we write the background parameter vector generically as
\begin{equation}
\bm{\theta}_{\rm bg} = (H_0,\Omega_m,\alpha_{\mathrm{rd}},\bm{\vartheta}_{\rm ST}),
\end{equation}
where \(\bm{\vartheta}_{\rm ST}\) denotes the scalar--tensor sector parameters (e.g.\ coupling and
potential parameters, and any nonequilibrium/source parameters), and \(\bm{\vartheta}_{\rm ST}=\emptyset\)
for Model A.

\paragraph{Observer-local imprint parameters (mapping layer).}
When the local imprint is enabled, we augment by a mapping parameter set
\begin{equation}
\bm{\theta}_{\rm map} = (s_{\mathrm{anchor}},\; \text{(optional shape parameters)}),
\end{equation}
with the fiducial analysis fixing the activation \emph{shape} and fitting only the amplitude
\(s_{\rm anchor}\) (Sec.~\ref{sec:earth_history2}).
The full parameter vector is
\begin{equation}
\bm{\theta} = (\bm{\theta}_{\rm bg},\bm{\theta}_{\rm map}).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Optimization and test statistic}
\label{sec:opt_stat}

We obtain best fits by direct numerical minimization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:chi2_tot} using a derivative-free method (Powell-type directional search) subject to physical bounds (e.g.\ $s_{\mathrm{anchor}}\ge 0$, $B>0$, $p\ge 0$) and numerical safeguards for monotonic inversion of $\zobs(\zcos)$.

Our primary global comparison statistic is the improvement
\begin{equation}
\dchi \equiv \chi^2_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}} - \chi^2_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM+LTA}},
\label{eq:dchi_def2}
\end{equation}
with $\dchi>0$ favoring LTA. In the representative run, we find $\dchi \simeq 13.7$.

To calibrate the null distribution of $\dchi$ beyond asymptotic $\chi^2$ approximations, we also perform Monte Carlo null injections (parametric bootstrap) under the baseline model using the same covariance and refitting both models. In an $N=10000$ null injection run, we obtain a Monte Carlo tail probability
$p_{\rm MC}\equiv P(\Delta\chi^2_{\rm null}\ge \Delta\chi^2_{\rm obs}) \approx 8\times 10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{Fiducial analysis choices.}
Unless otherwise stated, all quoted results use: (i) the Pantheon+SH0ES \texttt{shoes\_global}
selection (\(N_{\rm SN}=354\)) with the released full covariance, analyzed via the ladder-anchored
Schur-complement likelihood of Sec.~\ref{sec:sn_anchor_like} (calibrators retained as the absolute
anchor and the intercept marginalized using the calibrator posterior); (ii) the BAO consensus
compilation of Sec.~\ref{sec:data_bao} with a single sound-horizon scaling parameter
\(\alpha_{\rm rd}\), evaluated at observed redshift using the LTA mapping and Jacobian of
Sec.~\ref{sec:bao_like}; (iii) the correlated Planck-chain Gaussian prior on
\((H_0,\Omega_m,\alpha_{\rm rd})\) described in Sec.~\ref{sec:planck_prior} when explicitly stated;
and (iv) the LTA(powerlaw) activation family with fixed \(\tL=3.8\,{\rm Gyr}\), bounds
\(s_{\rm anchor}\ge0\), \(B>0\), \(p\ge0\), and \(\tA\) recomputed from the Hubble-flow subset only to
avoid leakage. Numerical inversion enforces \(\dd \zobs/\dd \zcos>0\) over the redshift domain of
interest.

\section{Primary Results}

This section reports the primary empirical outcomes of the \emph{observer-local mapping} analysis

Unless otherwise stated, results refer to run-tag \texttt{20260112-013808}, using:
(i) the Pantheon+SH0ES \texttt{shoes\_global} selection (\(N_{\rm SN}=354\), with
\(N_{\rm cal}=77\) calibrators and \(N_{\rm HF}=277\) Hubble-flow SNe; \(\zhd\in[0.00122,0.14898]\));

\(\chi^2_{\rm LTA}(s_{\rm anchor}=0)=\chi^2_{\Lambda{\rm CDM}}\).

\section{Robustness Tests}
\label{sec:robustness}

Because the LTA extension is designed to address a \emph{localized} structure in the low-$z$ Hubble
diagram, the natural failure modes are also localized: a single survey driving the effect, a
calibration artifact confined to a particular telescope, or a fit improvement that disappears once
correlated modes are treated consistently under resampling. We therefore emphasize robustness tests
that directly target (i) single-subset dominance, (ii) boundary-parameter significance calibration,
and (iii) the behavior of held-out scoring under strong train--test correlations.

\subsection{Jackknife resampling by telescope group (leave-one-group-out refits)}
\label{sec:jackknife}

A leading concern for low-redshift anomalies is that they may be driven by an instrument-specific
offset (photometric zero points, bandpass mismatches, selection peculiarities) rather than a
distributed kinematic feature of the local Hubble flow. To test this directly, we perform grouped
jackknife refits in which we remove all SNe associated with a given telescope/survey group,
refit \emph{both} baseline $\Lambda$CDM and $\Lambda$CDM+LTA(powerlaw), and record the improvement
\begin{equation}
\Delta\chi^2_{(-g)} \equiv
\chi^2_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM},(-g)} - \chi^2_{\mathrm{LTA},(-g)}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Protocol and leakage control.}
Each leave-out refit repeats the same likelihood treatment as the fiducial analysis:
(i) calibrators are retained to preserve the absolute-distance anchor; (ii) the anchored-likelihood
construction is recomputed consistently (including the calibrator posterior for $M$ and the Schur
complement conditioning of the Hubble-flow block); and (iii) the anchor time $\tA$ used to define
\(s_{\rm anchor}\) is recomputed from the remaining \emph{Hubble-flow subset only} (excluding
calibrators) to avoid leakage through the normalization. The optional modulation parameters are
fixed by construction in the fiducial configuration, so the extension degree of freedom is the LTA
amplitude \(s_{\rm anchor}\) only.

\paragraph{Result.}
Figure~\ref{fig:jackknife} and Table~\ref{tab:jackknife} summarize the leave-one-group-out results
for run-tag \texttt{20260112-013808}. The preference for LTA remains positive in all $9/9$ refits,
with
\begin{equation}
\Delta\chi^2_{(-g)} \in [12.27,\ 21.37], \qquad
\mathrm{median}(\Delta\chi^2_{(-g)})\simeq 13.73.
\end{equation}
Most notably, removing CfA (a large fraction of the older low-$z$ sample) \emph{increases} the
preference to \(\Delta\chi^2_{(-{\rm CfA})}\simeq 21.37\), which strongly disfavors the hypothesis
that the global improvement is an artifact of any single legacy dataset. The signal also persists
when removing high-precision modern samples (e.g.\ CSP and Foundation). The complete refit table
(including per-refit best-fit parameters and \(\tA\)) is saved by the pipeline as a CSV for direct
audit and reproduction.

\subsection{Parametric bootstrap (null injections) under the full released covariance}
\label{sec:null_bootstrap}

Because the LTA extension is nested and its null point lies on a boundary
(\(s_{\rm anchor}\ge 0\)), the null distribution of \(\Delta\chi^2\) is not guaranteed to follow a
simple \(\chi^2_1\) asymptotic law. We therefore calibrate the detection significance with a
parametric bootstrap (null injections) that exactly mirrors the data treatment and refitting
pipeline used on the real data.

\paragraph{Null-generating model.}
We take the best-fit baseline $\Lambda$CDM model (including the same BAO treatment and the same
prior choice used in the fiducial analysis) as the null. For each bootstrap realization we draw:
(i) a mock SN corrected-magnitude vector from a single multivariate Gaussian using the
\emph{released full covariance} on the selected \(N_{\rm SN}=354\) objects, preserving calibrator
cross-correlations; and (ii) a mock BAO vector from its provided covariance in the same observable
convention as Sec.~\ref{sec:data_bao}. For each mock we then reconstruct the anchored SN likelihood
exactly as in the real analysis: we recompute \((M_{\rm cal},\sigma_M)\) from the mock calibrators,
form the Schur complement covariance for the Hubble-flow block, and evaluate the cosmology-sensitive
objective via Eq.~\eqref{eq:chi2_hf}. The Planck prior (when enabled) is applied identically.

\paragraph{Bootstrap statistic.}
Each mock dataset is refit under both baseline $\Lambda$CDM and $\Lambda$CDM+LTA(powerlaw) using the
same optimizer, bounds, and inversion safeguards, yielding the bootstrap distribution of
\begin{equation}
\Delta\chi^2_{\rm boot} \equiv
\chi^2_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}} - \chi^2_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM+LTA}}
\end{equation}
evaluated at the best fits for that mock.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo calibrated tail probability.}
For an \(N=10000\) bootstrap run, with observed
\(\Delta\chi^2_{\rm obs}\simeq 13.65\), we obtain
\begin{equation}
\langle \Delta\chi^2_{\rm boot}\rangle \simeq 1.3549, \qquad
\mathrm{std}(\Delta\chi^2_{\rm boot}) \simeq 2.0011, \qquad
p_{\rm MC}\equiv P(\Delta\chi^2_{\rm boot}\ge \Delta\chi^2_{\rm obs}) \simeq 0.002.
\end{equation}
This Monte Carlo calibration directly answers the empirical question posed in this paper:
\emph{how often does a baseline-only Universe, observed with this covariance structure and analyzed
with this anchored-likelihood pipeline, produce an apparent preference as large as we observe?}
In these tests, such events are rare at the \(\sim 0.2\%\) level.

Figure~\ref{fig:null_injections} shows the empirical null distribution of \(\Delta\chi^2\) obtained by refitting both models to baseline-only mock realizations generated with the full released covariance.

\subsection{Cross-validation diagnostics under strong correlated covariance}
\label{sec:cv_summary}

We additionally report cross-validation (CV) as a \emph{diagnostic} of how the preference behaves
under held-out splits when the covariance contains strong long-range correlations. This is not a
replacement for the full-covariance likelihood-ratio evidence, but it is informative about whether
the improvement generalizes across survey blocks and about how correlated modes propagate across
train--test partitions.

\paragraph{Protocol (blocked by survey).}
We perform leave-one-survey-out blocked CV over \texttt{IDSURVEY} with \(k=15\) folds on the
Hubble-flow subset (\(N_{\rm HF}=277\)), keeping calibrators always in training to preserve the
absolute anchor. Fold sizes range from 1 to 72 (median 14). Within each fold we refit both baseline
and LTA on the training set and evaluate held-out scores on the test block. The anchor time \(\tA\)
is recomputed from the \emph{training} Hubble-flow subset only, ensuring that the amplitude
normalization cannot leak information from the held-out block.

\paragraph{Marginal versus conditional held-out scoring.}
When the joint data vector is Gaussian with nontrivial train--test cross-covariance, there are two
distinct, well-defined held-out scores:
(i) a \emph{marginal} score that evaluates the test residual using only the test covariance, and
(ii) a \emph{Gaussian conditional} score that conditions the test residual on the realized training
residual through the cross-covariance (Schur complement). Because Pantheon+SH0ES exhibits strong
correlations, these two scores can rank models differently; we therefore report both (Appendix
\ref{app:cv} gives the explicit definitions and their relation).

\paragraph{Observed CV behavior.}
For run-tag \texttt{20260112-013808}, summing over all held-out Hubble-flow objects across folds
gives:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\rm folds}\Delta\chi^2_{\rm test,marg} \simeq +113.724,\qquad
\sum_{\rm folds}\Delta\chi^2_{\rm test\mid train} \simeq -7.783,
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta\chi^2\equiv \chi^2_{\Lambda\mathrm{CDM}}-\chi^2_{\mathrm{LTA}}\) is computed on the
held-out block in each scoring convention. The marginal score favors LTA in \(10/15\) folds, while
the conditional score favors LTA in \(4/15\) folds.

This divergence is a characteristic signature of strong train--test correlations: the conditional
score effectively tests how well a model predicts the \emph{specific correlated realization} already
observed in training (and can therefore reward models that track that realization more closely),
whereas the marginal score measures unconditional held-out fit using only the test covariance.
In this setting, CV is most useful as a transparent diagnostic of correlation structure rather than
as the primary evidence metric. Our primary detection claim remains anchored to the global
full-covariance \(\Delta\chi^2\) and its parametric-bootstrap null calibration
(Sec.~\ref{sec:null_bootstrap}).

\subsection{CV-null injections (pilot calibration of the CV statistic)}
\label{sec:cv_null}

For completeness we also implement a CV-null procedure: generate baseline-only mocks and rerun the
entire CV pipeline to obtain a null distribution for the aggregated CV statistic. In the pilot run
with \(N=50\) null draws (seed 100), we obtain:
\begin{equation}
\text{Observed:}\ \Delta\chi^2_{\rm CV,marg} \simeq 113.72,\qquad
\Delta\chi^2_{\rm CV,cond} \simeq -7.78,
\end{equation}
and the null draws yield
\begin{equation}
\langle \Delta\chi^2_{\rm CV,marg}\rangle \simeq 2.61,\quad
\mathrm{std}\simeq 4.37;\qquad
\langle \Delta\chi^2_{\rm CV,cond}\rangle \simeq -0.75,\quad
\mathrm{std}\simeq 1.34.
\end{equation}
With only \(N=10\) null draws, the Monte Carlo resolution is coarse; in this pilot, none of the
null realizations exceed the observed marginal statistic, corresponding to the usual finite-sample
upper-bound estimator \(p\simeq 1/(N+1)=0.0909\). We therefore treat CV-null as a supplementary
calibration target and prioritize the higher-$N$ global bootstrap (Sec.~\ref{sec:null_bootstrap})
for the primary significance statement.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the data are telling us in plain terms}
The empirical pattern isolated by this analysis is simple: within the Pantheon+SH0ES ladder-anchored
likelihood (full released covariance, calibrator cross-correlations respected), the baseline
$\Lambda$CDM Hubble-flow residuals exhibit a coherent low-redshift structure (the ``red valley'')
concentrated at $z\lesssim 0.03$. The LTA mapping supplies a smooth, bounded, observer-anchored
distortion of the redshift--distance relation that preferentially acts at precisely these distances,
and it does so while leaving the best-fit \emph{background} parameters $(H_0,\Omega_m,\alpha_{\rm rd})$
nearly unchanged.

This combination matters: the improvement is not achieved by globally shifting the expansion history.
Instead, it is achieved by modifying the identification between \(\zobs\) and the background
\(\zcos\) in a way that is (i) causal by construction (Earth-retarded lookback time), (ii) bounded
(finite activation epoch), and (iii) testable in independent observables through the Jacobian that
enters radial BAO. In the present dataset combination, SNe drive the gain and BAO mildly penalizes
the mapping, which is exactly what one expects if the phenomenon is truly local rather than a
late-time change in \(\Hc(z)\) itself.

\subsection{A speculative physical motivation: organization and effective clock rate}
\label{sec:discussion_order}

General Relativity (GR) makes a sharp and experimentally verified statement: the rate at which
proper time accumulates depends on the spacetime metric. In weak fields this appears as gravitational
time dilation: clocks deeper in a gravitational potential well tick more slowly relative to clocks
farther out. In the standard cosmological analysis, such effects are treated through conventional
local corrections (peculiar velocities, gravitational potentials, etc.) and are not expected to
generate percent-level distortions of the global redshift--distance relation.

The Local Time Acceleration (LTA) hypothesis explored here is qualitatively different. It asks
whether there could exist an additional, observer-local contribution to effective clock rate that
correlates not primarily with mass density (as in GR), but with the \emph{degree of organization} of
the local environment. We emphasize at the outset that this is speculative: the present paper does
not derive such a coupling from established microphysics. Rather, it tests whether the
\emph{phenomenology} of an observer-anchored, causal, bounded redshift mapping is preferred by
late-time data when the published covariances are treated consistently.

A useful heuristic lens is provided by Stephen Wolfram's ``computational theory of time,'' in which
the passage of time is associated with the updating of relational structure in an underlying
discrete system. In that viewpoint, as the number of degrees of freedom and the density of their
interactions increase, the number of effective constraints or ``relationships'' can grow rapidly,
and one may expect the rate of consistent updating (i.e.\ the ``clock rate'' of the system) to be
reduced. Whether or not one accepts this computational framing literally, it suggests a concrete
intuition: time dilation can be viewed as arising when the local state is harder to update, in the
sense of having more entangled constraints among constituents.

The inverse question then becomes natural: if one increases \emph{order}---in the sense of reducing
the effective complexity of interactions by building persistent correlations and structured
organization---could an opposite-sign, observer-local effect arise in which the local pace of
physical processes is \emph{slightly faster} relative to a cosmological reference? In thermodynamic
language, living systems and engineered systems are paradigmatic examples of far-from-equilibrium
``negentropic bubbles'': they maintain low internal entropy (high organization) by consuming free
energy and exporting entropy to their surroundings, so that total entropy production remains
positive. The relevant speculative ingredient is therefore not a violation of the second law, but a
hypothesized coupling between \emph{local, sustained organization} (or some proxy such as long-lived
correlations, mutual information, or algorithmic complexity measures) and the rate at which local
proper time accumulates relative to a cosmological standard.

If such a coupling existed at a very small level, it could manifest observationally because
redshift is fundamentally a comparison between an emitted photon frequency and the receiver's local
frequency standard. Any systematic drift in the receiver’s effective clock rate relative to the
background time coordinate can be re-expressed as a multiplicative contribution to $(1+z)$, i.e.\ an
additive contribution to $\ln(1+z)$, precisely the structure implemented in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:zmap2}.

In the fiducial analysis we fix the cutoff epoch to $\tL=3.8\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ as a motivated
order-of-magnitude proxy for the timescale over which complex terrestrial biospheric organization
has existed. We emphasize that $\tL$ is \emph{not} inferred by the optimizer in the configuration
reported here; rather, the data show that a cutoff on this scale is compatible with, and can support,
a substantial fit improvement given the other model assumptions. Allowing $\tL$ (and the activation
shape parameters) to vary and mapping the resulting degeneracies with $s_{\rm anchor}$ is an
important extension left to future work.

If the observer-local interpretation of LTA were correct, it would not be a minor adjustment to
standard cosmological modeling. It would require extending our current framework in a way that
introduces an additional state variable (or effective degree of freedom) encoding some measure of
organization/order, rather than sourcing clock behavior solely through stress--energy. Constructing
a consistent covariant completion---and determining whether such a completion can be made compatible
with locality, Lorentz invariance, and precision metrology constraints---is an open theoretical
problem. In this paper we do not attempt that construction; we restrict ourselves to a minimal,
falsifiable phenomenological mapping and test it against SN+BAO data.

\paragraph{Thermodynamic closure and the role of clocks.}
In Jacobson’s thermodynamic derivation of general relativity, the Einstein field equations arise as a
local equilibrium condition enforcing the Clausius relation $\delta Q = T\,\delta S$ across all
local causal horizons \cite{Jacobson1995EinsteinEOS}. A complementary and thematically related viewpoint is the ``thermal time'' hypothesis, in which a
notion of time flow is associated with the statistical state of a generally covariant system via
modular structure rather than being taken as primitive \cite{ConnesRovelli1994ThermalTime}.
While our chronometric postulate is different in content, the shared motif is that the operational
time experienced by physical systems may be linked to coarse-grained state structure and
thermodynamic bookkeeping. In this framework, spacetime curvature is fixed
by the requirement that horizon entropy, taken to be proportional to area, evolves consistently
under local energy flux.

The Local Time Acceleration (LTA) hypothesis explored here is most naturally interpreted as a
\emph{nonequilibrium extension} of this picture. Sustained organization in driven subsystems does not
modify the equilibrium area--entropy relation directly, but instead introduces continuous entropy
production $\sigma = \nabla_\mu s^\mu \ge 0$ \cite{ElingGuedensJacobson2006Noneq}. Rather than sourcing
large curvature gradients, such nonequilibrium contributions can enter dominantly through the
\emph{clock sector}: they bias the local rate at which proper time accumulates relative to the
background cosmological time coordinate.

Operationally, redshift is a comparison between an emitted photon frequency and the observer’s local
frequency standard. A persistent, observer-local bias in clock rate therefore appears as a
multiplicative correction to $(1+z)$, independent of any large modification to null geodesics or
lensing. This motivates treating LTA primarily as an effective clock-rate perturbation sourced by
entropy production, with metric backreaction remaining subleading in the weak-field regime probed by
low-redshift supernovae.

\subsection{Earth budget closure: magnitude consistency with planetary free-energy throughput}
\label{sec:earth_budget_closure}

A central question for any observer-local, organization-sourced clock-bias hypothesis is whether
the inferred magnitude is even remotely compatible with known terrestrial energy and entropy
budgets. In our fiducial fit, the LTA mapping saturates at
\begin{equation}
I_{\rm sat} \simeq 2.67\times 10^{-3},
\end{equation}
corresponding to a fractional multiplicative factor in \((1+z)\) of
\(\exp(I_{\rm sat})-1 \approx 2.7\times 10^{-3}\) (a \(\sim 0.27\%\) effect).

In the constraint-field formulation developed in Appendix~\ref{app:lta_constraint_field}
(energy \(\rightarrow\) constraints \(\rightarrow\) clock-bias chain),
the relevant thermodynamic control parameter is not ``entropy'' in the sense of disorder, but the
\emph{nonequilibrium free-energy throughput that can be stored in, and continually used to maintain,
constraints} (i.e.\ microstate-space restrictions). The second law then supplies a robust bound:
maintaining (or increasing) any coarse-grained constraint inventory requires dissipation, and the
rate of constraint maintenance is bounded by available power and the effective dissipation
temperature.

\paragraph{Conservative biospheric throughput estimate.}
A conservative proxy for Earth’s sustained constraint-building throughput is the chemical free-energy
stored by global net primary production (NPP) \cite{Field1998NPP,BioNumbersNPP}.
Using a global NPP of
\(\dot M_C \sim 10^2\,{\rm PgC\,yr^{-1}}\) and a standard free-energy requirement
\(\Delta G^\circ \simeq 4.78\times 10^5\,{\rm J\,mol^{-1}}\) per mole of CO\(_2\) reduced to the
carbohydrate level \cite{Whitmarsh478kJ}, the implied biospheric free-energy throughput is of order
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm bio} \sim 10^{14}\ {\rm W},
\end{equation}
and the corresponding entropy-production scale at ambient temperature is
\begin{equation}
\dot S_{\rm bio} \sim \frac{P_{\rm bio}}{T_{\rm amb}}
\sim 10^{11}\ {\rm W\,K^{-1}}
\quad (T_{\rm amb}\sim 300\,{\rm K}).
\end{equation}
This is a \emph{throughput} bound: it does not assert that NPP equals total biospheric dissipation,
only that it sets a conservative scale for sustained, order-linked chemical free-energy processing.

\paragraph{Planetary entropy-production reference scale.}
A natural comparison scale for any Earth-sourced mechanism is the planetary entropy production
associated with the solar-to-infrared energy degradation of the Earth system, which is of order
\cite{Kleidon2010HierarchyMEP}
\begin{equation}
\dot S_{\oplus} \sim 10^{14}\ {\rm W\,K^{-1}}.
\end{equation}
Published radiative-entropy-budget estimates also report a smaller but still large internal
irreversible entropy-production scale,
\(\dot S_{\oplus,{\rm int}}\sim 10^{13}\,{\rm W\,K^{-1}}\),
associated with non-radiative irreversible processes within the climate system.

\paragraph{Closure in a dimensionless form.}
Because \(I_{\rm sat}\) is dimensionless, a minimal (and explicitly bounded) closure consistent with
second-law bookkeeping is to write the saturation depth as proportional to a dimensionless source
strength defined by an entropy-throughput fraction:
\begin{equation}
I_{\rm sat} \equiv \epsilon_{\rm tc}\,
\frac{\dot S_{\rm src}}{\dot S_{\rm ref}},
\label{eq:Isat_budget_closure}
\end{equation}
where \(\dot S_{\rm src}\) is the entropy-production throughput associated with the organized,
constraint-maintaining source region (conservatively \(\dot S_{\rm src}\sim \dot S_{\rm bio}\)),
\(\dot S_{\rm ref}\) is a planetary reference throughput (e.g.\ \(\dot S_{\oplus}\) or
\(\dot S_{\oplus,{\rm int}}\)), and \(\epsilon_{\rm tc}\) is a dimensionless transduction parameter
encoding the microphysical efficiency with which organized nonequilibrium throughput biases the
local clock sector.

With \(\dot S_{\rm bio}/\dot S_{\oplus}\sim 10^{-3}\) (radiative reference) or
\(\dot S_{\rm bio}/\dot S_{\oplus,{\rm int}}\sim 10^{-2}\) (internal reference),
the observed \(I_{\rm sat}\sim 2.7\times 10^{-3}\) corresponds to
\(\epsilon_{\rm tc}\) in the broad range \(\sim 10^{-1}\) to a few, i.e.\ not an astronomically
large or obviously impossible efficiency parameter. The important point is not that this fixes
\(\epsilon_{\rm tc}\), but that the \emph{required clock-bias amplitude is compatible, at the level
of order-of-magnitude energy/entropy throughput, with what Earth can sustain without violating the
second law.}

Full arithmetic and explicit numerical values used for the above ratios are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:earth_budget_numbers}.

\subsection{Spacetime interpretation: an observer-anchored frequency-drift imprint on the past light cone}
\label{sec:discussion_timewave}

Operationally, LTA is implemented as a multiplicative correction to the observed redshift,
\begin{equation}
1+\zobs = (1+\zcos)\exp[I(\chi)],
\end{equation}
with $I(\chi)$ defined as a line-of-sight integral of an effective drift-rate field $s(\chi)$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:I_def2}). This form is deliberately chosen because redshift factors compose
multiplicatively, while their logarithms add; an accumulated, small fractional frequency drift along
a trajectory naturally appears as an additive contribution to $\ln(1+z)$.

A physically transparent way to read this mapping is to recall that a redshift measurement compares
a received photon frequency to the receiver’s local time standard. If the receiver’s effective
clock rate is biased relative to a cosmological reference (for whatever reason), then the measured
frequency ratio acquires an extra factor that is indistinguishable from an additional redshift
contribution. In the present framework, that extra factor is encoded by $\exp[I(\chi)]$, where the
integral representation provides a controlled, causal way to localize the effect along the past
light cone and to enforce saturation.

The ``Earth-retarded lookback time'' argument $\tret$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:tret2}) then supplies a
specific causal bookkeeping device: it ties the strength of $s(\chi)$ at each point on the photon
path to a retarded-time assignment relative to the observer, with the intent that an observer-local
influence cannot affect regions outside its causal domain. In this language one may visualize the
activation as defining a retarded domain of influence expanding outward at (at most) the speed of
light. Incoming photons traverse this domain and accumulate a small net imprint in $\ln(1+z)$.
Because the activation is bounded and saturating, the accumulated imprint is also bounded: in the
fiducial best fit we reconstruct a saturation scale
$I_{\rm sat}\simeq 2.67\times 10^{-3}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:results_mapping}), i.e.\ an
$\mathcal{O}(10^{-3})$ multiplicative correction in $(1+z)$.

This picture is sometimes described colloquially as a ``time wave.'' We stress that nothing in the
analysis requires a literal propagating wave in a medium. Rather, it is a compact visualization of
a causal, observer-anchored domain within which an additional, small frequency-drift contribution
is accumulated. In the specific interpretation motivating the present model, the ``steps'' are not
physical displacements of the observer away from the sources, but incremental changes in the local
rate at which the observer’s clock accumulates proper time relative to the cosmological reference.
Photons emitted earlier then arrive at an observer whose effective time standard is slightly
different from what would be expected under baseline assumptions, and this mismatch appears as an
extra contribution to the measured redshift. The reason this can preferentially affect the
low-redshift Hubble diagram is precisely the bounded activation: the imprint grows with distance up
to a finite epoch, after which additional path length no longer increases $I(\chi)$.

\subsection{Relation to dark-energy phenomenology and broader implications}
\label{sec:discussion_de}

The analysis in this paper is intentionally scoped. The best-fit LTA profiles favored by the
Pantheon+SH0ES + BAO combination in our fiducial configuration are \emph{local and saturating}:
they are designed to act primarily in the low-$z$ regime where the Hubble ladder is anchored and
where coherent residual structure is observed, while becoming negligible at larger lookback times.
This is why the model can improve the low-$z$ Hubble-flow residuals without demanding a wholesale
rewrite of high-redshift cosmology.

With that said, it is difficult to discuss any redshift-mapping modification without acknowledging
the broader question it raises: could some portion of the phenomenology commonly attributed to
late-time acceleration be connected with assumptions about how observed redshift maps to the
background redshift used in distance predictions? In standard $\Lambda$CDM, dark energy (modeled as
a cosmological constant $\Lambda$) is inferred from the redshift dependence of distances and
expansion observables across a wide redshift lever arm. Our present implementation does not attempt
to re-fit that entire lever arm with a generalized mapping, and it does not claim to replace
$\Lambda$ as a successful effective description at high redshift. Indeed, in the data combination
used here, the most informative signature is that the improvement is localized to the nearby
Hubble flow and is already constrained by the Jacobian-sensitive radial BAO sector.

Nevertheless, if an organization-linked clock-rate bias were real in some form, one must at least
ask whether analogous effects could arise in other environments or epochs. For example, during
specific phases of structure formation---when baryons condensed into stars and galaxies and when
large, long-lived correlations emerged---it is conceivable (as a hypothesis to be tested) 
that the Universe exhibited different levels of sustained ``order'' than it does in the
late-time diffuse intergalactic medium. In such a scenario, photons from high-$z$ sources could
accumulate additional frequency-shift contributions as they traverse regions with different
organization histories, producing something akin to a ``redshift lens'' (a propagation-induced
frequency mapping) rather than a true modification of the background expansion rate. Whether any
realistic version of this idea can reproduce the suite of high-$z$ constraints (CMB peak structure,
high-$z$ BAO, growth of structure, lensing) is a stringent question, and nothing in our present
analysis answers it.

For clarity, we state our intent explicitly in the strongest terms. We chose not to resolve
both the Hubble tension and the existence of Dark Energy in one-shot, but if our result is to be
trusted then it opens the door to new explorations for alternatives to $\Lambda$CDM. In practice, the next
step would be to generalize the mapping (or its physical interpretation) beyond an Earth-anchored
activation, and then confront that generalization with the full high-redshift dataset hierarchy.
That program is well beyond the scope of the present work.

\subsection{A speculative aside: the Fermi paradox under ``time-acceleration wells''}
The Fermi paradox, like the Hubble tension, remains an open question in cosmological physics: if
life is common, why is clear evidence scarce? If negentropic time acceleration exists and scales
with sustained organization, then a sufficiently advanced civilization could inhabit a deeper local
``time-acceleration well'' than ours. In that scenario, outbound signals would need to propagate
out of that well, and from our perspective their society could appear ``in the future''—not because
information travels faster than light, but because their local proper-time accumulation per unit of
our coordinate time would be larger. This is speculative, but it illustrates a sharp point: an
LTA-like effect, if real and scalable, would have implications beyond cosmology, including how we
should model timebases and frequency drifts in SETI-style searches.

\subsection{Paths toward falsification with timing and metrology}
Because the LTA hypothesis is, at root, a claim about frequency comparisons and effective clock
rates, precision timekeeping is a natural arena for independent tests. Several possibilities are
worth highlighting, along with the corresponding difficulties:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{GNSS and terrestrial time-transfer networks.} GNSS clocks are continuously steered to a
coordinate time standard, and slow drifts are routinely absorbed into control models and treated as
systematics. This makes them challenging probes: a genuine slow local drift could be ``calibrated
away.'' Still, with access to sufficiently raw telemetry and careful modeling, one can search for
residuals that correlate with environmental or organizational proxies not captured by standard
relativistic corrections.

\item \textbf{Pulsar timing arrays (PTAs).} PTAs are exquisitely sensitive to timing residuals, but
they also inherit a fundamental ambiguity if the hypothesized effect couples to the receiver clock:
a drift in the terrestrial time standard is largely degenerate with broad classes of timing model
errors. In addition, pulsars and their magnetospheres are themselves highly structured systems, so
PTAs could in principle probe a superposition of source-side, propagation-side, and receiver-side
effects. This does not rule PTAs out; it highlights the need for careful null tests and independent
time standards.

\item \textbf{Differential optical clock experiments across controlled ``order'' environments.}
The most direct laboratory strategy is differential: compare two or more state-of-the-art optical
clocks (linked by fiber or transportable) placed in environments engineered to differ strongly in
their degree of sustained organization while controlling conventional influences (gravitational
potential, temperature, EM environment, pressure). Candidate environments include dense biospheres
(e.g.\ tropical rainforest sites versus deserts), high-throughput bioreactors with controlled
metabolic flux, and large-scale engineered information-processing systems (e.g.\ data centers).
The goal would be to bound (or detect) any reproducible differential drift beyond known systematics.

\item \textbf{Additional experimental directions.}
Other controlled comparisons that could be informative as timekeeping technology improves include:
(i) clocks operated in high-activity chemical plants or electrochemical storage facilities versus
quiet controls; (ii) clocks colocated with high-coherence electromagnetic systems (high-$Q$ cavities,
large interferometers) versus controls; (iii) deep underground laboratories versus surface sites to
change environmental noise and shielding; and (iv) long-duration campaigns designed to test
correlations with seasonal biosphere cycles, regional power throughput, or other measurable proxies
for sustained organization.
\end{itemize}

These tests are ambitious, but they have a decisive virtue: they are not cosmological, and they do
not rely on SN calibration pipelines. If an LTA-like effect is real and couples to local time
standards at the magnitude implied by the cosmological fit, precision metrology should either
constrain it sharply or reveal correlated residuals in a controlled setting. If, conversely, no
such signal exists in the laboratory, then the natural interpretation of the cosmological LTA
preference would shift toward unmodeled low-$z$ astrophysical systematics (local flows, selection,
calibration structure) rather than new clock-rate physics.

In summary, the conservative conclusion is that the present work isolates a specific empirical
pattern in the low-$z$ Hubble diagram that is well described by a bounded, causal, observer-anchored
mapping. Whether that mapping is merely an effective proxy for local kinematics or a hint of a
deeper connection between organization and time standards is not resolved here. What \emph{is}
resolved is the form of the question: the mapping makes concrete predictions for redshift Jacobians
and for clock/frequency drift observables, and those predictions can be tested---and potentially
falsified---by independent data.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have introduced and tested a phenomenological extension of flat $\Lambda$CDM in which the observed
redshift is related to the background cosmological redshift by a bounded, causal, observer-anchored
mapping. In this ``Local Time Acceleration'' (LTA) framework, photons accumulate a small additional
contribution to $\ln(1+z)$ along the line of sight via an effective drift rate $s(\chi)$ whose
amplitude is defined at an anchor time computed from the Hubble-flow subset only, and whose
activation decays to zero at a finite epoch. The model is nested: $s_{\rm anchor}\rightarrow 0$
recovers standard $\Lambda$CDM exactly.

Confronting this mapping with the Pantheon+SH0ES public release using the \emph{full} published
covariance (including calibrator--Hubble-flow cross-correlations) and a ladder-anchored
Schur-complement likelihood, and jointly fitting a BAO consensus compilation \cite{DESI2024BAO} (with the LTA Jacobian
properly propagated into the radial observable), we find a substantial improvement in global
goodness-of-fit relative to the baseline model in the fiducial configuration. In the representative
run reported here (run-tag \texttt{20260112-013808}), the best-fit LTA(powerlaw) model improves the
total objective by $\Delta\chi^2 \simeq 13.7$ while leaving the best-fit background parameters
$(H_0,\Omega_m,\alpha_{\rm rd})$ essentially unchanged. The gain is carried almost entirely by the
anchored Hubble-flow SN likelihood, with only a mild BAO penalty, consistent with a genuinely local
modification to the redshift--distance identification rather than a global change in $\Hc(z)$.

Residual and whitening diagnostics show that the improvement is not uniformly distributed in redshift:
it is localized to the very local volume ($z\lesssim 0.03$), where the baseline Hubble diagram
exhibits a coherent ``valley'' often discussed in the context of local kinematics. Within the LTA
framework, this structure is naturally modeled by a smooth, saturating mapping that becomes
negligible at higher redshift, yielding convergence to the standard relation for $z\gtrsim 0.05$.

Crucially, the preference is not fragile to single-subset removal. Grouped jackknife refits that
remove each major telescope/survey group in turn preserve a positive improvement in every case in
the tests reported here, disfavoring the hypothesis that the signal is driven by one faulty
instrument or one calibration subset. Because the LTA amplitude is bounded and the null point lies
on a parameter boundary, we further calibrate the detection statistic using parametric bootstrap
(null injections) that preserve the full released covariance and refit both models; with a
high-statistics run ($N=10000$ null draws) a tail probability of order $p\sim 0.0008$ was obtained for
a $\Delta\chi^2$ of this magnitude, and ongoing larger-$N$ runs are used to refine the Monte Carlo
resolution.

The present work is intentionally not a ``one-shot'' replacement for the standard cosmological model.
It establishes a narrower empirical claim: within a conservative full-covariance data treatment, a
bounded, causal redshift-mapping extension provides a better description of the low-$z$ Hubble
diagram than baseline $\Lambda$CDM in the configurations tested, and it does so in a way that is
robust to obvious single-survey explanations. Whether this mapping is best interpreted as a proxy
for unmodeled local kinematics (e.g.\ Local Void dynamics) or as evidence for a genuinely
observer-local clock-rate contribution remains open. The decisive next step is therefore
falsification via independent observables: precision clock networks, standard sirens, time-delay
cosmography, and sky-dependent correlations with local structure, all of which can sharply constrain
(or exclude) an LTA-like mechanism.

\[
\text{energy throughput} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{bounded constraint inventory} \;\Longrightarrow\;
\text{order field } \psi(x) \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{scalar--tensor coupling }F(\psi)
\;\Longrightarrow\; \text{(derived) chronometric factor }A(\psi)=F^{-1/2}
\]
and clarifies how these ingredients feed into two distinct observational channels:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Homogeneous cosmology:} the background mode \(\bar\psi(t)\) modifies the Jordan-frame
expansion history \(\HJ(z)\), enabling dark-energy-like effects without introducing \(\Lambda\) as a
separate component (Sec.~\ref{sec:jordan_background}).
\item \textbf{Observer-local imprint:} inhomogeneous perturbations \(\delta\psi(x)\) can generate
endpoint/integrated frequency-comparison effects that, in a controlled approximation, may be packaged
into an effective \(\Delta\ln(1+z)\) mapping (Sec.~\ref{sec:lta_mapping}).
\end{enumerate}

Everything up to the covariant insertion point \(\mathcal{S}_{\rm neq}\) uses standard
nonequilibrium/statistical thermodynamics and conservative bookkeeping. The nonstandard physics is
localized to: (i) the choice of scalar--tensor functions \(F(\psi)\), \(U(\psi)\), and (ii) the form
of the nonequilibrium source \(J_{\rm neq}\) that links sustained constraint-maintenance to the scalar
sector.

\paragraph{Conventions (fixed reference; GR recovery in vacuum).}
Throughout Appendix~\ref{app:foundation} we choose the maximum-entropy reference distribution $\pi$
\emph{once} and hold it fixed in time. Equivalently, the macroscopic conditions defining the
``unconstrained baseline'' (bath temperature $T$, energy function $E(\xi)$, conserved quantities,
etc.) are taken time-independent on the timescales of interest. With this convention the reference
equilibrium free energy $F_{\rm eq}$ is constant, and $\varphi$ can be interpreted as a true
``constraint inventory'' whose growth is bounded by input work (Sec.~\ref{app:second_law_bound}).

In the absence of matter/nonequilibrium sources (ideal vacuum), the local state coincides with the
reference ($p=\pi$), so $\varphi=\psi=0$ and realized clock time reduces to standard Jordan-frame proper time.
All LTA phenomenology is sourced by nonzero $\psi$ in material environments.


\subsection{A.1 Constraint inventory as microstate-space reduction}
\label{app:constraint_inventory}

We require a mathematically precise scalar quantifying ``constraint / order'' that:
(i) is nonnegative; (ii) behaves conservatively under coarse-graining; and (iii) has a direct
energetic meaning under the second law.

Let $\xi$ denote a microstate at a chosen coarse-graining and let $p(\xi,t)$ be the system’s
(coarse-grained) microstate distribution. Let $\pi(\xi,t)$ denote a reference maximum-entropy
distribution representing the \emph{unconstrained} baseline given whatever macroscopic constraints
we are \emph{not} counting as ``order'' (e.g.\ fixed energy, particle content, etc.). In the simplest
canonical case at temperature $T$, $\pi$ is Gibbs; more generally it can be a MaxEnt reference.

\paragraph{Definition (constraint inventory).}
Define
\begin{equation}
\phi(t) \equiv D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(p(\cdot,t)\,\Vert\,\pi\bigr)
=\int \dd \xi\,p(\xi,t)\ln\!\frac{p(\xi,t)}{\pi(\xi)}.
\label{eq:app_phi_def}
\end{equation}
and the entropy-unit inventory
\begin{equation}
\varphi(t)\equiv k_B\,\phi(t)
\qquad [\varphi]=\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}.
\label{eq:app_varphi_def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Core properties (why this is a conservative notion of ``order'').}
KL divergence satisfies:
\begin{align}
\varphi(t) &\ge 0, \quad \varphi(t)=0 \iff p=\pi\;\text{a.e.}, \label{eq:app_phi_nonneg}\\
\varphi(t) &\ge \varphi_{\rm coarse}(t) \quad \text{under any coarse-graining map (data-processing)}, \label{eq:app_phi_dataproc}\\
\varphi_{AB} &= \varphi_A+\varphi_B \quad \text{for independent composition } p_{AB}=p_Ap_B,\ \pi_{AB}=\pi_A\pi_B. \label{eq:app_phi_add}
\end{align}
Thus $\varphi$ is nonnegative, cannot be inflated by coarse-graining, and is additive under
independent composition.

\paragraph{Explicit microstate-space link.}
If $\pi$ is uniform on an allowed set of size $\Omega_0$ and $p$ is uniform on a constrained subset
of size $\Omega_1\le\Omega_0$, then
\begin{equation}
\phi = \ln\!\frac{\Omega_0}{\Omega_1}, \qquad
\varphi = k_B \ln\!\frac{\Omega_0}{\Omega_1}.
\label{eq:app_microstates}
\end{equation}
In this special case, $\varphi$ literally counts the log-reduction in accessible microstates.
This is the precise sense in which ``constraints reduce microstate space.''

\subsection{A.2 Nonequilibrium free energy: $T\varphi$ is a stored constraint budget}
\label{app:noneq_free_energy}

In an isothermal setting at temperature $T$, define the entropy functional
\begin{equation}
S[p]\equiv -k_B\int \dd \xi\, p(\xi,t)\ln p(\xi,t),
\label{eq:app_entropy}
\end{equation}
and the nonequilibrium Helmholtz free energy functional
\begin{equation}
F[p]\equiv \langle E\rangle_p - T S[p].
\label{eq:app_F_def}
\end{equation}
We assume the bath temperature $T$ and the energy function $E(\xi)$ defining the canonical reference are time-independent,
so that $F_{\rm eq}$ is a constant and $d(F-F_{\rm eq})/dt = dF/dt$.

For a canonical reference $\pi(\xi)\propto e^{-\beta E(\xi)}$ with $\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}$, the equilibrium
free energy is $F_{\rm eq}=-k_BT\ln Z$, and one has the standard identity
\begin{equation}
F[p]-F_{\rm eq} = k_B T\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert\pi)=T\,\varphi.
\label{eq:app_F_minus_Feq}
\end{equation}
In the fixed-reference convention adopted here, $F_{\rm eq}$ is constant in time.
Therefore $T\varphi$ is exactly the \emph{excess free energy above equilibrium} stored in the
maintained constraints/correlations relative to $\pi$.

\paragraph{Bits language (optional).}
Define $\varphi_{\rm bits}\equiv \varphi/(k_B\ln2)=\phi/\ln2$. Then $T\varphi$ corresponds to an
energy scale of $k_BT\ln2$ per bit, matching the Landauer scale for logically irreversible
operations. We do \emph{not} assume biological systems saturate this bound; it is used only as a
clean dimensional benchmark.

\subsection{A.3 Second-law throughput bound: power limits constraint growth}
\label{app:second_law_bound}

Assume an effective isothermal reservoir at temperature $T$.
Let $\dot Q$ be heat flow into the system and $\dot W$ work done on the system; the first law gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\langle E\rangle = \dot Q + \dot W.
\label{eq:app_firstlaw}
\end{equation}
Define total entropy production rate (system + environment) by
\begin{equation}
\dot\Sigma \equiv \frac{\dd S}{\dd t}-\frac{\dot Q}{T}\ \ge\ 0,
\label{eq:app_sigmadot}
\end{equation}
the local second law for this setting.

Using $F=\langle E\rangle-TS$ yields the standard balance
\begin{equation}
\dot W = \frac{\dd F}{\dd t} + T\dot\Sigma.
\label{eq:app_work_balance}
\end{equation}
Because the reference $\pi$ (hence $F_{\rm eq}$) and the bath temperature $T$ are fixed in the present convention,
combining \eqref{eq:app_F_minus_Feq} with \eqref{eq:app_work_balance} gives
\begin{equation}
T\,\dot\varphi = \frac{\dd}{\dd t}(F-F_{\rm eq}) \le \dot W \equiv P_{\rm in},
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\dot\varphi \le \frac{P_{\rm in}}{T}.
\label{eq:app_varphi_dot_bound}
\end{equation}
This is the key throughput bound used later: \emph{constraint inventory cannot grow faster than
available power divided by temperature.}

In steady maintenance regimes where $\langle \dot\varphi\rangle\simeq0$, one has
$\langle \dot\Sigma\rangle\simeq \langle P_{\rm in}\rangle/T$.

Convert to moles of carbon fixed per unit time using \(12\,{\rm g\,mol^{-1}}\):

\subsection{Biophysical mechanisms: how life maintains constraints on microstate space}
\label{sec:life_constraints}

This paper’s thermodynamic completion does not treat ``entropy'' as the fundamental measure of
organization. Instead, we define an explicit \emph{constraint inventory} \(\varphi\) (Appendix
\ref{app:foundation}) that quantifies how strongly a system’s coarse-grained microstate
distribution \(p\) is restricted relative to an unconstrained maximum-entropy reference \(\pi\),
\(\varphi = k_B D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert\pi)\).
Entropy production enters only as the \emph{accounting variable} demanded by the second law: it
bounds how quickly such constraints can be created or maintained by any physical process.

In this section we identify concrete, well-established biophysical mechanisms by which living
systems (cells, tissues, ecosystems) maintain \(\varphi>0\) for long durations by consuming free
energy and exporting entropy to their environment. These mechanisms provide a physically grounded
interpretation of the source term \(\sigma(x)\) that drives the order field \(\psi(x)\) in the
chronometric completion (Appendix \ref{app:foundation}), without assuming that biology violates the
second law or that ``entropy'' itself is the order variable. \cite{Seifert2012ST,Parrondo2015ThermoInfo}

\subsubsection{Life as a driven nonequilibrium steady state}
At biological scales, a cell is accurately modeled as an \emph{open, driven nonequilibrium system}:
it maintains concentrations, gradients, macromolecular structures, and correlations that would
relax toward equilibrium if energy throughput ceased. Mathematically, many such systems can be
represented at mesoscopic resolution by a stochastic Markov dynamics on states \(i\) with
probabilities \(p_i(t)\):
\begin{equation}
\dot p_i = \sum_j \bigl(W_{ij} p_j - W_{ji} p_i\bigr),
\end{equation}
where \(W_{ij}\) are transition rates.
At equilibrium with a reference distribution \(\pi\), rates satisfy detailed balance
\(W_{ij}\pi_j = W_{ji}\pi_i\). Living systems break detailed balance through continual chemical
driving (e.g.\ ATP/GTP hydrolysis, redox chemistry, ion pumping), creating sustained probability
currents and maintaining \(p\neq \pi\) (a nonequilibrium steady state) \cite{Seifert2012ST,Schnakenberg1976}.

The connection to our constraint inventory is direct: the nonequilibrium free energy stored in such
maintained constraints is \(F-F_{\rm eq}=T\varphi\) (Appendix \ref{app:foundation}).
Stochastic thermodynamics provides a standard energy/entropy balance for driven isothermal systems:
work input can be decomposed into the rate of change of nonequilibrium free energy plus a strictly
nonnegative entropy production term. This is the formal reason the second law constrains how large
and how rapidly maintained constraints can be \cite{Seifert2012ST,Parrondo2015ThermoInfo}.

\subsubsection{Mechanism 1: compartmentalization and boundary conditions (membranes)}
A defining physical act of life is the creation and maintenance of \emph{boundaries} that impose
conditional structure on accessible microstates. Lipid membranes and cell walls constrain spatial
degrees of freedom and enable long-lived gradients (ionic, chemical, electrical) that would quickly
disappear in an unconstrained fluid equilibrium. In the language of Appendix \ref{app:foundation},
these boundaries alter the feasible macrostate constraints defining \(\pi\) and maintain a \(p\)
that is concentrated on a restricted subset of microstates (thereby increasing \(\varphi\)).
The energetic cost arises because boundaries are not passive: maintaining them in the presence of
thermal fluctuations and chemical degradation requires continual repair, transport, and pumping,
all of which dissipate free energy and generate entropy production \cite{Phillips2012PBoC,Mitchell1961Chemiosmotic}.

\subsubsection{Mechanism 2: chemical potential gradients and chemostatted reaction networks}
At the biochemical level, the most universal ``constraint engine'' is a driven chemical reaction
network (CRN) with chemostatted species that act as fuel reservoirs (ATP/ADP/Pi, NADH/NAD\(^+\),
proton gradients, etc.). Such networks are a canonical setting of nonequilibrium thermodynamics:
one can define a nonequilibrium Gibbs free energy and show that maintaining a nonequilibrium state
requires work by the chemostats, with entropy production given by flux--affinity products:
\begin{equation}
\dot\Sigma = \sum_r \frac{J_r \mathcal{A}_r}{T}\ \ge\ 0,
\end{equation}
where \(J_r\) is the net flux of reaction channel \(r\) and \(\mathcal{A}_r\) its chemical affinity.
This formalism makes precise the statement that biological order is paid for by dissipation:
persistent organization corresponds to persistent nonzero affinities and fluxes, hence positive 
\(\dot\Sigma\) \cite{Seifert2012ST,Schnakenberg1976,Hill1977FreeEnergyTransduction}.

Crucially, this does \emph{not} imply that ``entropy'' is the order variable. In our chain, order is
\(\varphi\) (a microstate-restriction measure), while \(\dot\Sigma\) is the second-law bookkeeping
term that bounds how \(\varphi\) can change:
\begin{equation}
\dot\varphi \le \frac{P_{\rm in}}{T},
\end{equation}
as derived in Appendix \ref{app:foundation}. A cell’s free-energy throughput is therefore a direct
upper bound on how quickly it can build or refresh microstate restrictions, independent of any 
philosophical interpretation of entropy \cite{Seifert2012ST,Parrondo2015ThermoInfo}.

\subsubsection{Mechanism 3: polymer sequence order and kinetic proofreading (energy for specificity)}
A large fraction of biological ``constraints'' are not merely concentration gradients, but
\emph{sequence-level correlations} in polymers: DNA/RNA sequences, folded proteins, and regulated
macromolecular complexes. These are strong restrictions on microstate space because they represent
highly specific arrangements among astronomically many alternatives.

A key point for rigor is that biology achieves error rates that exceed what would be expected from
equilibrium discrimination alone, by using energy-driven \emph{kinetic proofreading}. In Hopfield’s
mechanism, an irreversible, energy-consuming step (e.g.\ via phosphate hydrolysis) allows the system
to reject wrong intermediates more often than equilibrium would permit, increasing specificity at a
thermodynamic cost. This is an explicit, mathematically analyzable example of
\[
\text{energy consumption} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{conditional pathway filtering} \;\Rightarrow\;
\text{reduced accessible outcome microstates},
\]
i.e.\ an increase in maintained constraint inventory \(\varphi\) \cite{Hopfield1974Proofreading,Ninio1975Proofreading}.

\subsubsection{Mechanism 4: feedback control and information processing}
Living systems constantly sense and regulate internal variables (chemotaxis, gene regulation,
homeostasis). In physics terms, this is feedback control on stochastic degrees of freedom. A robust
result across the thermodynamics-of-information literature is that \emph{reliable} information
processing and irreversible operations have thermodynamic costs (Landauer scale for erasure), and
more generally that feedback-controlled systems must still satisfy second-law inequalities when
information flows are accounted for. In our framework, the relevance is structural: feedback loops
implement \emph{conditional constraints} on trajectories through state space, and maintaining those
conditionals at low error requires dissipation, contributing to \(\dot\Sigma\) and thereby bounding
maintainable \(\varphi\). \cite{Parrondo2015ThermoInfo,Landauer1961,Bennett1982}

\subsubsection{From local constraints to a cosmology-facing source term}
The previous subsections justify, in standard biophysical terms, why life plausibly sources a
positive nonequilibrium throughput field \(\sigma(x)\) (or an equivalent proxy such as local
dissipated power density divided by temperature) that can be used as the causal source of an
order field \(\psi(x)\) in Appendix \ref{app:foundation}. Importantly, \(\sigma(x)\) is not a
synonym for order: it is the second-law production term that accompanies the building and
maintenance of constraints.

In this language, ``life alters microstate space in front of every moment'' can be stated
mathematically and without mysticism: by consuming free energy, living systems actively reshape the
transition structure (the effective generator \(W\)) of local chemical and mechanical degrees of
freedom \cite{Seifert2012ST}, thereby maintaining a state distribution \(p\) that is persistently
restricted relative to the unconstrained reference \(\pi\).
The maintained restriction is quantified by \(\varphi=k_B D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert\pi)\)
\cite{KullbackLeibler1951,CoverThomas2006}, its rate of refresh is bounded by power through
\(\dot\varphi\le P_{\rm in}/T\) \cite{Seifert2012ST}, and in the chronometric completion this
throughput sources \(\psi(x)\), which (by postulate) biases realized clock time and thereby maps
\(z_{\rm obs}\) to \(z_{\rm cos}\) without modifying photon trajectories or large-scale geometry
\cite{Wald1984GR,Faraoni2004ST}.

\label{app:early_universe:frames}

We start from the Jordan-frame scalar--tensor completion
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:JF_action} or Eq.~\eqref{eq:U_action}), where \(\gJ_{\mu\nu}\) is the physical metric
seen by matter and by the photon--baryon plasma.

\paragraph{Jordan \(\rightarrow\) Einstein transformation.}
Define the Einstein-frame metric
\begin{equation}
\gE_{\mu\nu}\equiv \Fpsi\,\gJ_{\mu\nu},
\qquad
\Apsi \equiv \Fpsi^{-1/2},
\qquad
\Cpsi \equiv \ln\Apsi=-\frac12\ln\Fpsi.
\label{eq:W_conformal}
\end{equation}
In Einstein variables, matter couples to \(\Apsi^2\gE_{\mu\nu}\). A convenient special case is
\(F(\psi)=e^{-2\epsilon\psi}\Rightarrow \Apsi=e^{\epsilon\psi}\), but the bookkeeping below is valid
for general \(F(\psi)\).

\paragraph{Scale factors and Hubble rates.}
For homogeneous FLRW,
\begin{equation}
ds_{\mathrm{E}}^2=-d\tE^2+\aE^2(\tE)d\bm{x}^2,
\qquad
ds_{\mathrm{J}}^2=-d\tJ^2+\aJ^2(\tJ)d\bm{x}^2,
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\aJ = \Apsi\,\aE,
\qquad
d\tJ = \Apsi\,d\tE.
\end{equation}
Therefore the physical (matter-frame) expansion rate relevant to recombination is
\begin{equation}
\HJ \equiv \frac{1}{\aJ}\frac{d\aJ}{d\tJ}
=
\frac{1}{\Apsi}\left(\HE + \frac{d\ln\Apsi}{d\tE}\right),
\label{eq:W_HJ_HE}
\end{equation}
where \(\HE\equiv \dot\aE/\aE\) and the derivative is with respect to \(\tE\).

\paragraph{Observed redshift in the homogeneous limit.}
In the homogeneous FLRW limit, the physically observed redshift is
\begin{equation}
1+z \equiv \frac{\aJ(\tJ_0)}{\aJ(\tJ)}.
\end{equation}
Expressed in Einstein variables this becomes
\begin{equation}
1+z = \frac{\Apsi_0\,\aE(\tE_0)}{\Apsi\,\aE(\tE)},
\label{eq:W_zobs_general}
\end{equation}
which is the usual Einstein-frame expression with an endpoint \(\Apsi\) factor, but here it is not a
new postulate: it is algebraically equivalent to the Jordan-frame scale-factor law.

The remaining subsections use Einstein-frame equations as a computational convenience while keeping
track of which combinations (\(\HJ\), \(a_J\), \(r_d\)) correspond to physical matter-frame observables.

\label{eq:W_einstein_eq}

with
\begin{equation}
T^{(\psi)}_{\mu\nu}
=
f^2\nabla_\mu\psi\nabla_\nu\psi

g_{\mu\nu}\left[\frac{f^2}{2}(\nabla_{\mathrm{J}}\psi)^2 + f^4U(\psi)\right].
\label{eq:W_Tpsi}
\end{equation}

The scalar equation of motion is
\begin{equation}
f^2\,\Box\psi - f^4U'(\psi)

\qquad
J_{\rm neq}\equiv -\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S_{\rm neq}}{\delta\psi}.
\label{eq:W_scalar_eom}

For $A=e^{\epsilon\psi}$, $d\ln A/d\psi=\epsilon$:
\begin{equation}
f^2\,\Box\psi - f^4U'(\psi)
=
-\epsilon\,T_{(m)} + J_{\rm neq}.
\label{eq:W_scalar_eom_eps}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{FLRW background.}
Take Einstein-frame FLRW
\begin{equation}
ds^2 = -dt^2 + a^2(t)\,d\vec x^2,
\qquad
H\equiv \frac{\dot a}{a},
\qquad
\psi=\bar\psi(t),
\label{eq:W_FLRW}
\end{equation}
and define scalar energy density and pressure (Einstein frame)
\begin{equation}
\rho_\psi = \frac{f^2}{2}\dot{\bar\psi}^{\,2} + f^4U(\bar\psi),
\qquad
p_\psi = \frac{f^2}{2}\dot{\bar\psi}^{\,2} - f^4U(\bar\psi).
\label{eq:W_rho_p_psi}
\end{equation}
Then the Friedmann equations are
\begin{align}
3M_{\rm Pl}^2H^2 &= \rho_\psi + \rho_m + \rho_r + \rho_{\rm neq},
\label{eq:W_friedmann1}\\
-2M_{\rm Pl}^2\dot H &= (\rho_m+p_m)+(\rho_r+p_r)+(\rho_\psi+p_\psi)+(\rho_{\rm neq}+p_{\rm neq}).
\label{eq:W_friedmann2}
\end{align}

The homogeneous scalar equation from \eqref{eq:W_scalar_eom_eps} is
\begin{equation}
f^2\left(\ddot{\bar\psi}+3H\dot{\bar\psi}\right)+f^4U'(\bar\psi)
=
-\epsilon\,\bar T_{(m)} + \bar J_{\rm neq}(t),
\label{eq:W_psi_background}
\end{equation}
where $\bar T_{(m)}\equiv -\rho_m+3p_m$ (Einstein-frame trace).

\subsection{W.4 Energy-momentum exchange and density scalings}

Ignoring $Q^\nu_{\rm neq}$ for the early-time plasma (a controllable assumption; reinstate if needed),

\begin{equation}
\dot\rho_i + 3H(\rho_i+p_i)
=
-\epsilon\,\dot{\bar\psi}\,(-\rho_i+3p_i)
=
+\epsilon\,(1-3w_i)\,\dot{\bar\psi}\,\rho_i.
\label{eq:W_rho_exchange}
\end{equation}
Thus:
\begin{align}
\text{Radiation }(w=1/3):\quad &\dot\rho_r + 4H\rho_r = 0,\qquad \rho_r\propto a^{-4},

In the matter frame, densities follow the standard scalings with $\tilde a$:

\label{eq:W_tilde_scalings}
\end{equation}
consistent with $1+z=\tilde a_0/\tilde a$.

\subsection{W.5 Sound speed, sound horizon, and the drag scale $r_d$}
\label{app:early_universe:rd}

The baryon--photon sound speed is (matter frame)
\begin{equation}
c_s(z) = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3(1+R(z))}},
\qquad
R(z)\equiv \frac{3\tilde\rho_b(z)}{4\tilde\rho_\gamma(z)}.
\label{eq:W_cs}
\end{equation}
Using the standard matter-frame scalings $\tilde\rho_b\propto (1+z)^3$ and $\tilde\rho_\gamma\propto(1+z)^4$,
\begin{equation}
R(z)=R_0(1+z)^{-1},
\qquad
R_0\equiv \frac{3\tilde\rho_{b0}}{4\tilde\rho_{\gamma0}}.
\label{eq:W_Rz}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Sound horizon at an arbitrary redshift.}
Define the comoving sound horizon at redshift $z$ by
\begin{equation}
r_s(z) \equiv \int_{z}^{\infty}\frac{c_s(z')}{\tilde H(z')}\,dz'.
\label{eq:W_rs_def}
\end{equation}
Equivalently in scale factor,
\begin{equation}
r_s(\tilde a) = \int_{0}^{\tilde a} \frac{c_s(\tilde a')}{\tilde a'^2\,\tilde H(\tilde a')}\,d\tilde a'.
\label{eq:W_rs_a}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Drag epoch $z_d$ and $r_d$.}
The BAO ruler relevant for late-time BAO analyses is the sound horizon at the \emph{drag epoch} $z_d$,
when Compton drag ceases to efficiently couple baryons to photons. A clean definition is via a drag optical depth:
the baryon-photon momentum-exchange rate is proportional to $\dot\tau/R$, where $\tau$ is Thomson optical depth.
Define
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\tau}{d\eta} = \tilde a\,n_e\,\sigma_T\,c,
\qquad
R(\eta)=\frac{3\tilde\rho_b}{4\tilde\rho_\gamma},
\label{eq:W_tauprime}
\end{equation}
and the drag optical depth
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\rm drag}(z)
\equiv \int_{z}^{\infty}
\frac{c\,\sigma_T\,n_e(z')}{(1+z')\,\tilde H(z')}\,
\frac{1}{R(z')}\,dz'.
\label{eq:W_tau_drag}
\end{equation}
Then $z_d$ is defined implicitly by
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\rm drag}(z_d)=1,
\label{eq:W_zd_def}
\end{equation}
and the BAO sound horizon is
\begin{equation}
r_d \equiv r_s(z_d)=\int_{z_d}^{\infty}\frac{c_s(z')}{\tilde H(z')}\,dz'.
\label{eq:W_rd_def}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{What changes relative to $\Lambda$CDM.}
The microphysics in $c_s$ is unchanged in the matter frame, but the \emph{entire} early calibration changes
through $\tilde H(z)$, which is modified by:
(i) the order-field energy density in \eqref{eq:W_friedmann1}--\eqref{eq:W_friedmann2}, and
(ii) the chronometric relation \eqref{eq:W_tildeH} linking $H$ and $\tilde H$ via $\dot{\bar\psi}$.

Therefore, to replace $\alpha_{\rm rd}$ with a derived $r_d$ in a global fit, one must solve the background
system \eqref{eq:W_friedmann1}--\eqref{eq:W_psi_background} and then compute \eqref{eq:W_zd_def}--\eqref{eq:W_rd_def}.

\subsection{W.6 Recombination: $x_e(z)$, optical depth, and the visibility function}
\label{app:early_universe:recombination}

The recombination-era observables needed for CMB prediction are controlled by the free-electron fraction
$x_e(z)\equiv n_e/n_H$ and the Thomson optical depth $\tau(z)$.

\paragraph{Thomson optical depth.}
Define the Thomson optical depth (from $z$ to today) by
\begin{equation}
\tau(z) \equiv \int_{0}^{z}
\frac{c\,\sigma_T\,n_e(z')}{(1+z')\,\tilde H(z')}\,dz',
\label{eq:W_tau_def}
\end{equation}
where $n_e(z)=x_e(z)\,n_H(z)$ and $n_H(z)$ is the hydrogen number density.

\paragraph{Visibility function and photon decoupling.}
The visibility function is
\begin{equation}
g(z) \equiv e^{-\tau(z)}\frac{d\tau}{dz},
\qquad
\frac{d\tau}{dz} = \frac{c\,\sigma_T\,n_e(z)}{(1+z)\,\tilde H(z)}.
\label{eq:W_visibility}
\end{equation}
The recombination redshift $z_\*$ (last scattering) is conventionally defined as the peak of $g(z)$
(or approximately where $\tau(z_\*)\sim 1$); in a full computation, $z_\*$ emerges from $x_e(z)$ and $\tilde H(z)$.

\paragraph{Recombination ODE: schematic Peebles/Recfast form.}
A standard effective recombination equation can be written schematically as
\begin{equation}
\frac{dx_e}{dz}
=
\frac{1}{(1+z)\,\tilde H(z)}
\left[
\mathcal{R}(z)\,x_e^2\,n_H(z)
-
\mathcal{I}(z)\,(1-x_e)
\right]\,
\mathcal{C}(z),
\label{eq:W_xe_general}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item $\mathcal{R}(z)$ is an effective case-B recombination coefficient (depends on $T_m\simeq T_\gamma$ prior to thermal decoupling),
\item $\mathcal{I}(z)$ is an effective photoionization coefficient,
\item $\mathcal{C}(z)$ is the Peebles ``$C$ factor'' encoding Lyman-$\alpha$ trapping and 2s--1s two-photon decay.
\end{itemize}
The \emph{only} structural change forced by the unified order field at this level is the replacement
\[

\]
in the Hubble-dilution/time-conversion factor $1/[(1+z)\tilde H(z)]$.
(If one instead works in Einstein frame, then particle masses and rates become $\psi$-dependent and the
microphysical coefficients acquire additional $A(\psi)$ factors; the matter-frame formulation avoids that.)

\paragraph{Temperature scaling.}
Because $1+z=\tilde a_0/\tilde a$, the photon temperature obeys the standard scaling in terms of the observed redshift:
\begin{equation}
T_\gamma(z) = T_0(1+z).
\label{eq:W_T_scaling}
\end{equation}
Thus the CMB monopole scaling remains standard in the \emph{fully self-consistent} matter-frame cosmology.
What changes is the mapping between redshift and cosmic time via $\tilde H(z)$.

which is the same structural statement as the curvature--entropy relations derived from the informational

\section{Pulsar timing in the chronometric/order-field theory:
source term, propagation through space, and receiver term}
\label{app:pta_full}

This appendix derives the timing observable relevant for pulsar timing arrays (PTAs) in the
chronometric/order-field framework, explicitly including:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item the pulsar's local clock sector (``their time hole''; \textbf{source term}),
\item propagation through interstellar space (``empty space''; \textbf{transfer}),
\item and the Earth's local clock sector (``our time hole''; \textbf{receiver term}).
\end{enumerate}
The resulting structure naturally yields a \emph{pulsar term} and an \emph{Earth term} analogous to
the standard PTA response to gravitational waves, but here the underlying physics is a scalar
chronometric potential $\mathcal{C}(x)=\ln A(\psi)$ rather than a tensor metric perturbation.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{P.1 Chronometric potential and local clock times}
\label{app:pta:clock_times}

Let the chronometric factor in Einstein variables be the standard derived factor
\begin{equation}
\Apsi \equiv \Fpsi^{-1/2},
\qquad
\mathcal{C}(x)\equiv \ln \Apsi(x) = -\frac12\ln \Fpsi(x).
\label{eq:P_C_def}
\end{equation}
Along any worldline, the physical (Jordan) clock time is related to Einstein proper time by
\begin{equation}
d\tau_{\mathrm{J}} = \Apsi\,d\tau_{\mathrm{E}} = e^{\mathcal{C}}\,d\tau_{\mathrm{E}}.
\label{eq:P_tau_relation}
\end{equation}
A commonly used special case is \(F(\psi)=e^{-2\epsilon\psi}\Rightarrow \Apsi=e^{\epsilon\psi}\), but
the PTA bookkeeping below does not require fixing a particular functional form.

We will need two worldlines:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item Pulsar worldline $P$: $\mathcal{C}_P(t)\equiv \mathcal{C}(x_P(t))$ and $d\tilde\tau_P = e^{\mathcal{C}_P}d\tau_P$.
\item Earth/receiver worldline $\Eclk$: $\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)\equiv \mathcal{C}(x_{\Eclk}(t))$ and $d\tilde\tau_{\Eclk} = e^{\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}}d\tau_{\Eclk}$.
\end{itemize}

In PTA practice one models times in a barycentric coordinate time; here we keep the derivation
covariant and then specialize to a weak-field/slow-motion regime where coordinate time is an
adequate affine label.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{P.2 Pulse phase and the observable: arrival-time residuals}
\label{app:pta:phase_toa}

Let $N$ be the pulse phase (integer pulse count). By definition,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dN}{d\tilde\tau_P}=\nu_P(\tilde\tau_P),
\label{eq:P_phase_def}
\end{equation}
where $\nu_P$ is the pulsar spin frequency measured in the pulsar's own clock time.
In the simplest case $\nu_P\simeq \nu_0$ is constant plus known secular spindown that PTA models already fit.

A pulse emitted at event $x_P$ propagates along a null ray to be received at $x_E$.
Let $t$ label receiver coordinate time and $t_P(t)$ the corresponding emission coordinate time
(the standard retarded relation; in flat space $t_P=t-D/c$ up to Shapiro and kinematic corrections).

The key PTA observable is the \emph{timing residual} $R(t)$: the difference between observed pulse
arrival times and the best-fit timing model (which includes standard GR propagation terms, ephemeris,
dispersion, spindown, etc.). Any additional fractional frequency shift $z(t)$ contributes as
\begin{equation}
R(t)=\int^t z(t')\,dt',
\label{eq:P_residual_def}
\end{equation}
up to the convention of which time coordinate is used in the integral (in practice one uses the same
reference time variable as the timing model). The derivation below gives $z(t)$ induced by $\mathcal{C}$.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{P.3 Instantaneous observed frequency including both source and receiver clocks}
\label{app:pta:freq_ratio}

The arrival phase rate measured by the receiver clock is
\begin{equation}
\nu_{\rm obs}(t)\equiv \frac{dN}{d\tilde\tau_{\Eclk}}.
\label{eq:P_nuobs_def}
\end{equation}
Using the chain rule,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dN}{d\tilde\tau_{\Eclk}}
=
\frac{dN}{d\tilde\tau_P}\,
\frac{d\tilde\tau_P}{dt_P}\,
\frac{dt_P}{dt}\,
\frac{dt}{d\tilde\tau_{\Eclk}}.
\label{eq:P_chainrule}
\end{equation}
Now apply the clock relations $d\tilde\tau=e^{\mathcal{C}}d\tau$.
In a weak-field slow-motion regime (appropriate for PTA baselines), we can take $dt\simeq d\tau$ along each worldline
to leading order in standard gravitational potentials; all such GR terms are already part of the timing model.
Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\tilde\tau_P}{dt_P}\simeq e^{\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)},
\qquad
\frac{dt}{d\tilde\tau_{\Eclk}}\simeq e^{-\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)}.
\label{eq:P_clock_factors}
\end{equation}
Also $dt_P/dt\simeq 1$ up to conventional kinematic/gravitational propagation corrections (again part of the baseline model).
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
\nu_{\rm obs}(t)
\simeq
\nu_P(\tilde\tau_P)\,
e^{\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)-\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)}.
\label{eq:P_nuobs_main}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Interpretation in the ``three-stage'' picture you requested.}
Equation \eqref{eq:P_nuobs_main} factorizes cleanly:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Flickering at a rate based on their time (source).}
Any variability in $\nu_P(\tilde\tau_P)$ and/or $\mathcal{C}_P$ is defined in pulsar clock time $\tilde\tau_P$.

\item \textbf{Exiting their time hole $\rightarrow$ traveling across empty space (transfer).}
If the interstellar path has negligible $\mathcal{C}$ (empty space: $A\simeq 1$), then the only
chronometric contribution from the pulsar environment is the \emph{endpoint value} $\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)$.
This is exactly the ``climb out of their local time hole'' factor $e^{+\mathcal{C}_P}$.

\item \textbf{Entering our time hole (receiver).}
Upon reception, the measured frequency is divided by our local clock factor $e^{\mathcal{C}_E(t)}$.
This is the receiver ``entering our time hole'' factor $e^{-\mathcal{C}_E}$.
\end{enumerate}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{P.4 Fractional frequency shift: Earth term + pulsar term}
\label{app:pta:redshift}

Define the fractional frequency shift relative to a baseline in which $\mathcal{C}\equiv 0$:
\begin{equation}
z_\mathcal{C}(t)
\equiv
\frac{\nu_{\rm obs}(t)-\nu_{\rm obs}^{(0)}(t)}{\nu_{\rm obs}^{(0)}(t)}.
\label{eq:P_z_def}
\end{equation}
If $\nu_P$ is treated as already modeled (i.e.\ we isolate the additional chronometric effect),
then from \eqref{eq:P_nuobs_main} we have
\begin{equation}
1+z_\mathcal{C}(t) \simeq e^{\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)-\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)}.
\label{eq:P_z_exact}
\end{equation}
For small perturbations (the PTA-relevant regime),
\begin{equation}
z_\mathcal{C}(t) \simeq \mathcal{C}_P(t_P)-\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)
= \epsilon\bigl[\psi_P(t_P)-\psi_{\Eclk}(t)\bigr].
\label{eq:P_z_linear}
\end{equation}
This is the PTA analogue of the standard ``pulsar term minus Earth term'' structure:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\text{PTA chronometric redshift}:\quad
z_\mathcal{C}(t)\ \propto\ \psi_P(\text{retarded})\ -\ \psi_E(\text{now}).
}
\end{equation}

The corresponding timing residual is
\begin{equation}
R_\mathcal{C}(t)
=
\int^t \left[\mathcal{C}_P(t_P(t'))-\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t')\right]dt'
\quad\text{(to leading order)}.
\label{eq:P_residual_main}
\end{equation}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{P.5 ``Flickering based on their time'': mapping pulsar-time variability into our time}
\label{app:pta:flicker}

Your explicit requirement is that any PTA equations must correctly include the fact that a process
that is periodic in \emph{pulsar clock time} will be observed through the combined
(source clock $\rightarrow$ propagation delay $\rightarrow$ receiver clock) mapping.

Let a pulsar-local chronometric modulation be specified as a function of pulsar clock time:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_P = \widehat{\mathcal{C}}_P(\tilde\tau_P),
\qquad
d\tilde\tau_P = e^{\mathcal{C}_P(t)}\,dt
\quad(\text{weak-field }dt\simeq d\tau_P).
\label{eq:P_Cp_of_tautilde}
\end{equation}
Then the pulsar term in \eqref{eq:P_z_linear} is evaluated at the retarded emission time:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)=\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_P\!\bigl(\tilde\tau_P(t_P)\bigr),
\qquad
\tilde\tau_P(t_P)=\int^{t_P} e^{\mathcal{C}_P(t')}dt'.
\label{eq:P_retarded_mapping}
\end{equation}

To express the observed modulation in \emph{our} clock time $\tilde\tau_{\Eclk}$,
use $d\tilde\tau_E=e^{\mathcal{C}_E(t)}dt$, so
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\tilde\tau_P}{d\tilde\tau_{\Eclk}}
=
\frac{e^{\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)}}{e^{\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)}}\,
\frac{dt_P}{dt}.
\label{eq:P_time_warp}
\end{equation}
To leading order $dt_P/dt\simeq 1$, therefore
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\tilde\tau_P}{d\tilde\tau_{\Eclk}}
\simeq
e^{\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)-\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)}.
\label{eq:P_time_warp_simple}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Observed flicker rate.}
If the pulsar modulation is sinusoidal in pulsar time,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_P(\tilde\tau_P)=\mathcal{C}_{P0}+\delta\mathcal{C}_P\cos(\omega_P\tilde\tau_P),
\label{eq:P_sine}
\end{equation}
then the instantaneous angular frequency measured in receiver clock time is
\begin{equation}
\omega_{\rm obs}(\tilde\tau_{\Eclk})
=
\omega_P\,\frac{d\tilde\tau_P}{d\tilde\tau_{\Eclk}}
\simeq
\omega_P\,e^{\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)-\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)}.
\label{eq:P_omega_obs}
\end{equation}
This equation is the mathematically explicit version of your statement:
\begin{quote}
\emph{``Flickering at a given rate based on their time (needs to exit their local time hole to reach us),
traveling across empty space, reaching us and then entering our local time hole.''}
\end{quote}
The pulsar-time frequency $\omega_P$ is warped by the ratio of clock factors between emission and reception.
If the path is empty space ($\mathcal{C}\approx0$ along the transfer), the only warp is the endpoint difference.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{P.6 Allowing an extended ``time-hole'' profile and propagation domains}
\label{app:pta:extended}

If the pulsar (or Earth) time-hole is spatially extended, then $\mathcal{C}(x)$ varies with radius.
In the conformal-chronometric theory, the net chronometric contribution to a frequency comparison is still
an endpoint difference in $\mathcal{C}$:
\begin{equation}
\ln\left(\frac{\nu_{\rm meas}}{\nu_{\rm meas}^{(0)}}\right)
=
-\left[\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}-\mathcal{C}_P\right],
\label{eq:P_endpoint_difference}
\end{equation}
because $\mathcal{C}$ is a scalar and $\int_\gamma d\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{\rm obs}-\mathcal{C}_{\rm emit}$.
Operationally, ``exiting the pulsar time hole'' is precisely the factor $e^{+\mathcal{C}_P}$ when the asymptotic region has $\mathcal{C}\to0$.

If, however, one wants to \emph{model} $\mathcal{C}(x)$ as a causal field sourced by the local nonequilibrium throughput,
then $\mathcal{C}$ itself must be computed from a retarded Green’s function (Appendix~\ref{app:tret_green} generalization):
\begin{equation}
\psi(x) \;=\; \psi_{\rm hom}(x)
+\kappa\int d^4x'\sqrt{-g}\,G_{\rm ret}(x;x')\,\sigma(x'),
\qquad \mathcal{C}(x)=\epsilon\psi(x).
\label{eq:P_retarded_field}
\end{equation}
In that representation, both Earth and pulsar have their own retarded source histories, and the PTA observable
$z_\mathcal{C}(t)$ samples \emph{two} distinct causal solutions at two distinct worldlines:
\begin{equation}
z_\mathcal{C}(t)\simeq \epsilon\left[\psi_P\bigl(t_P(t)\bigr)-\psi_{\Eclk}(t)\right].
\label{eq:P_two_worldlines}
\end{equation}

\subsection{P.7 Practical PTA implication: monopole Earth-term degeneracy and the role of the pulsar term}
\label{app:pta:pta_implications}

Equation \eqref{eq:P_z_linear} has an important structural consequence:
the Earth term $-\mathcal{C}_{\Eclk}(t)$ is a \emph{common} contribution to all pulsars, i.e.\ a monopole in the PTA.
This is (partially) degenerate with terrestrial clock errors unless it is separated by:
(i) independent time standards, (ii) pulsar-term structure, or (iii) sky-dependent propagation/anisotropy.

The pulsar term $\mathcal{C}_P(t_P)$ is different for each pulsar and thus offers a handle to break degeneracy,
but it enters at a retarded time (set by the pulsar distance and standard propagation effects), exactly as in
the gravitational-wave PTA response.

\section{Cross-validation under strong covariance coupling}
\label{app:cv}

This appendix reports cross-validation (CV) diagnostics for transparency, but it also explains why
CV is \emph{not} used as the primary evidentiary statistic in this analysis.

The key technical point is that the Pantheon+SH0ES release used here is not even approximately an
i.i.d.\ dataset: the published covariance contains long-range, survey-spanning correlated modes
(including calibrator--Hubble-flow cross-blocks and broad systematics). In that regime, the
statistically correct object for model comparison is the \emph{global} likelihood with the full
covariance, and the statistically correct calibration for a bounded, nested extension is the null
distribution of the global likelihood-ratio statistic obtained by parametric bootstrap
(Sec.~\ref{sec:opt_stat} and Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness}). Cross-validation answers a different
question that depends sensitively on how one treats train--test cross-covariance; it is therefore
best viewed as a \emph{diagnostic of covariance-leveraging and subset sensitivity}, not as a
replacement for the global full-covariance comparison.

\subsection{Why CV is not the primary statistic here}
\label{app:cv:why}

For a Gaussian data vector with known covariance, the full-data log-likelihood is (up to an additive
constant)
\begin{equation}
-2\ln \mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}) \equiv \chi^2(\bm{\theta})
= \bm{r}(\bm{\theta})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{C}^{-1}\bm{r}(\bm{\theta}),
\end{equation}
with residual vector $\bm{r}$ and the published covariance $\bm{C}$.
This is the canonical quantity used throughout precision cosmology: it is exactly the statistic
that correctly weights and de-correlates the measured modes, including modes that couple widely
separated objects and surveys.

Cross-validation, by contrast, is most naturally justified when one has many approximately
independent draws from a common distribution and one is primarily concerned with ``generalization''
to new samples.
Pantheon+SH0ES is not of that type. It is one correlated realization of a single Universe observed
through a complex inference pipeline with shared calibration and selection systematics encoded in
$\bm{C}$. A CV split does not manufacture new independent information; it partitions a single
correlated draw.
As a result, CV scores can be dominated by \emph{how correlated modes are apportioned across folds}
rather than by whether a model provides a better global description of the realized dataset.

More concretely: let the data be partitioned into train (T) and test/validation (V),
\begin{equation}
\bm{r}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{r}_T\\
\bm{r}_V
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\bm{C}=
\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{C}_{TT} & \bm{C}_{TV}\\
\bm{C}_{VT} & \bm{C}_{VV}
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The Gaussian \emph{conditional} predictive distribution implied by the same covariance model is
\begin{equation}
\bm{r}_V \mid \bm{r}_T \sim
\mathcal{N}\!\Bigl(\bm{C}_{VT}\bm{C}_{TT}^{-1}\bm{r}_T,\ 
\bm{C}_{V\mid T}\Bigr),
\qquad
\bm{C}_{V\mid T}\equiv \bm{C}_{VV}-\bm{C}_{VT}\bm{C}_{TT}^{-1}\bm{C}_{TV},
\label{eq:cv_cond_gauss}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding conditional held-out score is
\begin{equation}
\chi^2_{V\mid T}(\bm{\theta})
=
\bigl(\bm{r}_V-\bm{C}_{VT}\bm{C}_{TT}^{-1}\bm{r}_T\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}
\bm{C}_{V\mid T}^{-1}
\bigl(\bm{r}_V-\bm{C}_{VT}\bm{C}_{TT}^{-1}\bm{r}_T\bigr).
\label{eq:cv_cond_score}
\end{equation}
Many ``standard'' CV implementations in the literature instead evaluate a \emph{marginal} held-out
score that ignores train--test cross-covariance,
\begin{equation}
\chi^2_{V,\mathrm{marg}}(\bm{\theta}) \equiv \bm{r}_V^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{C}_{VV}^{-1}\bm{r}_V.
\label{eq:cv_marg_score}
\end{equation}
When $\bm{C}_{VT}\neq \bm{0}$ (as is emphatically true here), these are different statistics with
different meanings, and they can rank models differently even when computed on the same split.

The full-data likelihood ratio $\Delta\chi^2$ used in the main text is not ``in conflict'' with CV:
it is answering the physically and statistically relevant question for this problem---\emph{which
model better explains the single correlated dataset under the published covariance model?}---and it
is calibrated by null injections that preserve that same covariance structure. CV is included for
robustness and transparency, but it is not a more rigorous substitute for the global statistic in
the strong-correlation regime.

\subsection{CV protocol used in this work}
\label{app:cv:protocol}

We perform blocked CV over the \emph{Hubble-flow} subset only, while always retaining calibrators in
training. This choice is forced by the ladder-anchored likelihood construction:
calibrators provide the absolute-distance anchor and define the intercept posterior used to
marginalize $M$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:sn_anchor_like}). Leaving calibrators out of training would replace
a physics-motivated anchored likelihood with a different statistical problem and would not test the
same model.

For the representative run reported in the main text (run-tag \texttt{20260112-013808}), the split
is:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item Calibrators: $N_{\mathrm{cal}}=77$ kept in train for all folds.
\item Hubble-flow pool: $N_{\mathrm{HF}}=277$ partitioned into $k=15$ folds.
\item Blocking: leave-one-survey-out folds over \texttt{IDSURVEY} (15 surveys), i.e.\ each fold holds
out one survey block (sizes: min 1, median 14, max 72).
\item Seed: \texttt{cv\_seed=100}.
\item Anchor normalization: $\tA$ is recomputed from the training Hubble-flow subset only in each
fold to avoid leakage of held-out information into the LTA amplitude normalization.
\end{itemize}

For each fold we:
(i) refit baseline $\Lambda$CDM and $\Lambda$CDM+LTA(powerlaw) on the training set using the same
anchored likelihood construction and numerical safeguards as in the full-data analysis; and
(ii) evaluate held-out scores on the test block using both the marginal score
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv_marg_score}) and the Gaussian conditional score (Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv_cond_score}),
with the \emph{full} train--test cross-covariance retained (\texttt{cond\_cross=full} in the
pipeline).

\subsection{Why we report both ``marginal'' and ``conditional'' held-out scores}
\label{app:cv:both}

In a strongly correlated Gaussian model, there is no single CV number that is universally
appropriate unless one states explicitly what is being conditioned on.

The marginal score $\chi^2_{V,\mathrm{marg}}$ asks: \emph{given the parameters fit on training, how
large are the held-out residuals under the test-block covariance alone?} This resembles the
classical out-of-sample score used in weakly dependent problems.

The conditional score $\chi^2_{V\mid T}$ asks a sharper question: \emph{given the parameters fit on
training and given the realized training residual field, how surprising is the held-out residual
once we regress out the part linearly predictable from the training residuals under the published
cross-covariance?} This is the correct predictive statistic if one treats the covariance model as
the generative model and one genuinely wants $p(\bm{y}_V \mid \bm{y}_T)$.

Because Pantheon+SH0ES includes large-amplitude survey-spanning modes, conditioning can remove a
substantial fraction of the variance in $\bm{r}_V$; in that regime, $\chi^2_{V\mid T}$ can become a
measure of the model's performance on \emph{residual structure orthogonal to the correlated modes
already realized in training}. That is a valid diagnostic, but it is not the same question as global
model preference under the full likelihood, and it should not be used to dismiss a full-data
likelihood-ratio detection that is itself calibrated under the same covariance assumptions.

\subsection{CV-null injections}
\label{app:cv:null}

We additionally perform CV-null injections as a sanity check on the CV statistics themselves:

\end{equation}
while the null draws yielded:
\begin{align}
\Delta\chi^2_{\rm CV,marg,null} &: \ \text{mean}=1.179420,\ \text{std}=3.846321,

\Delta\chi^2_{\rm CV,cond,null} &: \ \text{mean}=-0.242446,\ \text{std}=1.004216,
\ \ p_{\rm MC}\approx 1.
\end{align}
Here the reported $p_{\rm MC}$ is the Monte Carlo tail probability
$P(\Delta\chi^2_{\rm null}\ge \Delta\chi^2_{\rm obs})$ estimated from the finite null sample.
With $N=50$ CV-null draws, none exceeded the observed marginal CV statistic, giving
$p_{\rm MC}\approx 1/(50+1)=0.0196$ at this Monte Carlo resolution.

The CV-null exercise is included to show, explicitly, that the CV statistics behave as expected
under the assumed covariance model:
the marginal CV statistic can display broad dispersion under correlated systematics and can, in
finite samples, register large positive totals, while the conditional CV statistic is anchored close
to its null mean once the predictable correlated modes are regressed out.
This is precisely why CV is not elevated to the primary evidentiary role in this paper: it is not
measuring the same object as the global full-covariance likelihood ratio, and in the strong-coupling
regime its interpretation depends on conditioning choices that are orthogonal to the main
cosmological question addressed here.

\subsection{Connection back to the main result}
\label{app:cv:connection}

The central evidentiary quantity in this work is the \emph{global} improvement in the full-data
Gaussian objective,
\(\Delta\chi^2=\chi^2_{\Lambda{\rm CDM}}-\chi^2_{\Lambda{\rm CDM+LTA}}\),
evaluated using the anchored SN likelihood that respects the published Pantheon+SH0ES covariance
(including calibrator--Hubble-flow cross-covariances) and combined with BAO and the optional Planck
prior as stated in the main text. Because the LTA extension is nested with a boundary null
(\(s_{\rm anchor}\ge 0\)), the interpretation of \(\Delta\chi^2\) is based on an empirical null
distribution obtained by parametric bootstrap (null injections) that mirrors the same covariance
structure and refitting protocol.

The cross-validation calculations reported in this appendix serve a different purpose. In a dataset
with strong survey-spanning covariance, train--test partitions do not produce independent draws;
they redistribute correlated modes between folds. As a result, held-out scores depend sensitively
on whether one evaluates test residuals marginally (using \(\bm{C}_{VV}\) alone) or conditionally
(with the full train--test cross-covariance retained). Reporting both scores is therefore primarily
a transparency exercise: it demonstrates how correlation structure propagates through blocked
splits, and it provides a diagnostic check that the observed improvement is not confined to a
single survey block under the marginal scoring convention.

Accordingly, the CV results should be read as \emph{diagnostics of subset stability and covariance
coupling}, not as an alternative evidence metric that supersedes the full-covariance likelihood
ratio. The main conclusion of the paper remains anchored to the global \(\Delta\chi^2\) computed on
the complete dataset under the published covariance model and to its Monte Carlo calibration under
baseline-only mocks.
